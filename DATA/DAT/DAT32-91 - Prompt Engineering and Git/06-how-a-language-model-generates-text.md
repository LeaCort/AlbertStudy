6. How a Language Model Generates Text
To prompt a model well you need a working picture of what it is doing. A large language model
(LLM) does one thing, over and over: given the text so far, it predicts the next token and appends
it, then repeats with the new text as input. A token is a chunk of text — often a word or a piece of
a word. That is the whole engine. Everything else — chat, tools, reasoning — is built on top of this
single loop.
Definition 4 (Next-token prediction). Given a sequence of tokens (the context), a language
model outputs, for every token in its vocabulary, a score saying how likely that token is to come
next. Text is generated by turning those scores into probabilities, choosing one token, appending
it to the context, and repeating.


## 6.1. From scores to a choice: softmax and temperature

which makes every value positive and makes them sum to one:

## 6.2. Trimming the tail: top-p sampling


Even a flattened distribution has a long tail of barely-plausible tokens, and occasionally choosing one
produces nonsense. Top-p sampling (also called nucleus sampling) guards against that: before
choosing, it keeps only the smallest set of most-likely tokens whose probabilities add up to at least
𝑝, and discards the rest. On our 𝑇 = 1 distribution, top-p = 0.9 works like this:

The running sum crosses 0.9 at cold, so the five tokens up to and including cold are kept and the
three least-likely tokens are dropped entirely — they cannot be chosen this step. Top-p keeps variety
where the model is genuinely uncertain (many tokens survive) but clamps down where it is confident
(few survive), which temperature alone does not do.


## 6.3. Why the same prompt gives different answers


Now the payoff. Once a distribution is set, the model samples from it — it draws a token at
random according to the probabilities. Because that draw is random, running the same prompt again
can produce a different token, and therefore a different continuation. Temperature controls how
different. Sampling twelve times from our distribution at two temperatures makes this visible:
The prompt is the same every time. Only the sampled token changes.
T = 0.7 (focused): cloudy, sunny, sunny, sunny, warm, rainy, cloudy, cloudy, sunny,
cold, warm, sunny
T = 1.3 (adventurous): warm, sunny, sunny, sunny, cold, windy, warm, rainy, warm, hot,
cold, sunny
The prompt was identical every time. At 𝑇 = 0.7 the samples stay near the top of the distribution —
the run never leaves the group cloudy, sunny, warm,
rainy, cold. At 𝑇 = 1.3 the same twelve draws reach windy and hot, tokens the focused run never
visits. That is the mechanism behind an experience you will have constantly: ask a chatbot the
same thing twice and get two different answers. It is not a bug — it is sampling from a probability
distribution, tuned by temperature and top-p.
Principle 5 (The generation loop, in one breath). Predict logits for the next token → soften
or sharpen them with temperature → optionally trim the tail with top-p → sample one token →
append it → repeat. Randomness at the sampling step is why the same prompt can give different
outputs; temperature and top-p are the dials that control how much it varies.
Pitfall. Lower temperature does not mean “more correct” — it means “more repeatable”.
Pushed to its limit, 𝑇 → 0 becomes greedy decoding: always take the single most likely token.
(This is what “𝑇 = 0” is shorthand for — the limit as the temperature shrinks, not a number you
substitute into the softmax, which divides by 𝑇 and is undefined at zero.) A confidently wrong
