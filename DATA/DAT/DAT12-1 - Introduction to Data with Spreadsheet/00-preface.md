Preface
Welcome to the first course of your data education. It is, on purpose, the most humble one. Before
you train a model, query a database, or write a line of Python, you will spend this whole course inside
a tool you already half know: the spreadsheet. No prior experience is assumed. What is assumed is
a willingness to slow down.
The one habit this course builds is short to say: look at the data before you touch it. A beginner
reaches straight for a formula, a chart, an average. Experienced data workers first do something that
looks like nothing — they scroll, they sort, they read a column, they ask “does this even make sense?”
— before computing anything. They have learned that a number computed from dirty data is worse
than no number, because it looks confident while being wrong; and that a chart answering the wrong
question misleads exactly as much as a wrong calculation.
A spreadsheet is the right place to learn this, because everything is visible. Nothing is hidden in a
pipeline. Every value sits in a cell you can click on, and every common mistake — a number stored
as text, a repeated row, a stretched axis — is right there on the screen, once you have trained your
eye to see it. That training is the real subject of the course. By the end you will be able to take a real
business question, answer it using only spreadsheet operations, and write down your work so that a
colleague can repeat it without asking you anything.
Remark (How to read this book). The step-by-step boxes use the names common to the
three widely used spreadsheets — Microsoft Excel, Google Sheets, and LibreOffice Calc. Where a
name differs between them, both are given. The example money amounts use a neutral currency
unit written ¤; nothing in any analysis depends on which real currency it stands for. Dates are
written in the international YYYY-MM-DD form (for example 2026-01-04) so they read the same
everywhere.
