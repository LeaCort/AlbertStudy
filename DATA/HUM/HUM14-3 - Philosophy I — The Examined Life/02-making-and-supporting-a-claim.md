2. Making and supporting a claim
Questioning opens a problem. Sooner or later you have to answer it — to put forward a claim and
stand behind it. This chapter is about doing that well: stating a hypothesis and supporting it with
reasons whose inferences actually hold.


## 2.1. What an argument is, and what it is not


Example — two ways of disagreeing. Two students disagree about a course of action. The first
says: “I just feel it is wrong. I cannot explain why, but I do.” The second says: “It is wrong because
it treats a loyal person as a disposable cost, and treating people as disposable is wrong.” Only the
second has made an argument. She has stated a general principle (treating people as disposable
is wrong), a claim about the case (this action does that), and a conclusion (so it is wrong). You can
now engage her: you can challenge the principle, or deny that the case fits it. The first student has
given you nothing to engage. You can only agree or shrug.
Definition 3. An argument is a set of premises offered in support of a conclusion. The
premises are the claims taken as starting points; the conclusion is the claim they are meant to
establish; an inference is the step from the premises to the conclusion. When you put a claim
forward for discussion and set out to support it, that claim is your hypothesis. A report of a
feeling, an assertion with no support, or a description of how something feels is not an argument,
and cannot be examined as one.
We insist on arguments rather than opinions for a practical reason: only an argument can be
examined. An opinion can be shared or not shared, and that is the end of it. An argument has parts,
and each part can be inspected.

An argument can fail at any of these three places: a false premise, a faulty inference, or a conclusion
that does not follow even when each premise is granted. Chapter 3 turns each of these into a way of
objecting.


## 2.2. Supporting a claim, and making its presuppositions explicit


A hypothesis is only as good as what holds it up. You support a claim with reasons (premises that
give grounds for it) and examples (cases that show the claim doing real work). But an example is
not yet an argument: it illustrates a claim, it does not establish it. The example must be connected to
the conclusion by an inference that holds.

The most important discipline in building an argument reaches back to Chapter 1: make your own
presuppositions explicit. Every argument rests on premises it does not state. Stating them is not a
weakness; it is what lets the argument be examined honestly, by you first of all.
Example — the hidden premise. “You should take the promotion — it pays far more.” Stated
fully, this argument has a hidden premise: “you should take whatever pays far more.” Once
surfaced, the hidden premise looks far less obvious than the original one-liner, and that is the point.
A good arguer surfaces it themselves, and either defends it or narrows the claim (“you should take
it if the extra pay is worth what the role costs you”).


## 2.3. Valid and sound


Start with an argument that is watertight and worthless at once.
Example — airtight, yet it tells us nothing. “All billionaires are happy. Smith is a billionaire.
Therefore Smith is happy.” The reasoning is beyond reproach: if both premises were true, the
conclusion would be forced. Yet the first premise is false — plenty of billionaires are miserable —
so the conclusion tells us nothing about Smith. The argument is flawless in its logic and useless in
its result.
Two words let you say precisely what has gone right and what has gone wrong here.
Definition 4. An argument is valid if its conclusion would follow supposing the premises were
true — the reasoning holds. It is sound if it is valid and its premises are in fact true. Validity is
about the reasoning alone; soundness adds the demand that the starting points be true.
The billionaires argument is valid but not sound: its reasoning holds, but a false premise sinks it.
Validity is necessary but not enough — a watertight inference from a false premise is still worthless.
Aim for soundness in your own arguments: true premises and inferences that hold. When you build
a hypothesis, aim to make it survive the three checks of Chapter 3 — checks you run on yourself
before anyone else runs them on you.
Method 2 (building a defensible hypothesis).
1. State the claim precisely, sharply enough that one could argue both for and against it.
2. Give the reasons — the premises that support it — and at least one example where the claim
does real work.
3. Surface your own presuppositions, especially the hidden premise the argument needs but
does not say.
4. Check each inference, and check that the conclusion does not claim more than the premises
license.
Pitfall. The most common substitute for an argument is a vivid example with no inference
attached. “Look at Jobs — he dropped out and changed the world, so formal education is
overrated.” One case is not a general claim, and the leap from “it worked for one famous person”

Exercises
2.1. Here is an argument. “Reading fiction makes people more empathetic. Empathetic people
make better managers. So companies should encourage their managers to read fiction.” Name
the two premises and the conclusion.
2.2. Consider: “Every successful entrepreneur takes risks. Maria takes risks. Therefore Maria is
a successful entrepreneur.” Is this argument valid? Is it sound? Explain, using the terms of this
chapter.
2.3. “You should move to the city — that is where all the opportunities are.” State the hidden
premise this argument needs, then say whether, once surfaced, it is as obvious as the original
one-liner made it sound.
2.4. Take a claim about success you actually hold — for example, “it is better to be excellent at
one thing than good at many.” Write it as a hypothesis: state the claim, give one reason and one
example, and surface the presupposition your argument depends on.
2.5. Build the most defensible short argument you can for the conclusion “a secure job is worth
more than a higher-paying but precarious one.” Then name your own weakest premise — the
one an opponent would attack first.
Answers. (1) Premise 1: reading fiction makes people more empathetic. Premise 2: empathetic people make better
managers. Conclusion: companies should encourage managers to read fiction. (2) The argument is invalid, so it
cannot be sound. Even if every successful entrepreneur takes risks, it does not follow that everyone who takes risks is
a successful entrepreneur — most risk-takers are not. The inference runs the premise backwards. (3) Hidden premise:
“you should go where the opportunities are (whatever they cost you).” Surfaced, it is far less obvious — it ignores what
leaving costs, and whether the opportunities are ones you want. (4) Self-check: a good answer states a real reason
(not the claim restated), gives a genuine example, and names an assumption the argument needs — for instance,
that depth is more valuable than breadth in the field you have in mind, which is not true everywhere. (5) Self-check:
a strong answer gives true, relevant premises and an inference that holds, and then honestly points to the premise
most open to attack (often the implicit claim that security is worth the earnings given up), rather than declaring every
premise obvious.
