7. Constructing and defending a thesis on
success
This book ends where the practice ends: in a defended personal thesis on what it means to succeed.
Here every earlier skill is put to work at once — questioning, arguing, objecting, conceptualising,
interpreting, and the five criteria. It is judged, like everything here, only on the rigour of the
reasoning, never on whether the reader shares your conclusion.


## 7.1. What a rigorous thesis contains


Method 6 (the five parts of a rigorous thesis).
1. A clear claim about what it means to succeed, stated precisely enough that one could argue
both for and against it.
2. Two thinkers in productive tension: two of the five criteria used together, so that their
disagreement does real work, rather than one cited as a decorative authority.
3. A serious counter­argument: the strongest objection to your claim, stated in its most
forceful form.
4. An answer to that objection, delivered and then defended against further objection —
revising the claim where the objection is sound, holding it with reasons where it is not.
5. The consequences drawn: what your account of success, once it has survived objection,
asks of a life — the choices it forces, the comforts it removes, what it commits you to and what
it rules out. A thesis that says what success is and never what follows from it has done only
half the work.
Below is a worked thesis carrying all five parts. Study its shape; then the exercises ask you to build
one of your own.
Example — a full worked thesis.
1. The claim.
“To succeed is to build a life organised around increasing your genuine power to act. Status and
recognition are worth pursuing only where they also increase that power, and not for their own
sake.”
2. Two thinkers in productive tension.
Spinoza supplies the core: joy is the increase of power to act, so a life aimed at that is a life aimed at
joy. But Aristotle pushes back from within the argument: eudaimonia, a flourishing life, plausibly
needs external and social goods too — friends, standing, some security — which pure “power to
act” seems to discount. The thesis lives in that tension. Seneca is used to reveal how much of what
we call “status” is in fact borrowed approval that would not survive his subtraction.
3. The strongest counter­argument, stated as if I held it.
“This thesis is a comfortable idea that only suits people who are already secure. For many people,
genuine power to act depends on exactly the status it wants to set aside: money, networks, recog-
nition and security are what let a person act at all. To tell them to pursue power to act rather than

status is to tell those who are already secure to enjoy a freedom that those who are not secure
cannot afford. Aristotle is right that flourishing needs external goods, and Spinoza’s inner power
is a luxury.”
4. The answer, and the defence.
I concede the true part, and it is a large one: status that buys real capability — security that lets you
take risks, a network that lets you act, money that buys time — genuinely increases power to act,
and my thesis welcomes it. For someone not yet secure, pursuing exactly that money and security
is pursuing power to act, and my thesis endorses it without reservation. So where the objection
insists that external goods matter, it is not attacking my thesis; it is restating a part of it.
The objection’s charge — “a luxury only the secure can afford” — nonetheless rests on a hidden
premise: that my thesis tells people to set status aside, to pursue power to act instead of the money
and security they need. That premise is false (check 1 of Chapter 3): the thesis was never “reject
all status”; it was “pursue status only where it also increases your power to act.” Read correctly, it
asks the insecure to do precisely what the objection says they must — seek the means to act — so
the charge does not land. So I revise the claim to state openly that it welcomes capability-buying
status for everyone, those not yet secure most of all, which sharpens it rather than abandoning
it; and I hold the core against the charge, once its false premise is removed. What survives is a
sharper thesis: seek the status that enlarges what you can do — whoever you are and wherever
you start — and be suspicious, with Seneca, of the status you want only because the crowd wants
it for you.
5. The consequences drawn.
Take the sharpened thesis seriously and it makes concrete demands. It tells me to weigh any
prestigious offer by a test the salary does not answer — will this role enlarge what I can actually
do, or only how I look? — and to be ready to decline the impressive job that shrinks me. It tells
me to treat security and connections as instruments, banked for the capability they buy and not
hoarded as trophies. And it asks something genuinely costly: to give up the steady reassurance of
the external scoreboard, and to run Seneca’s subtraction on my own ambitions often enough that
it stays uncomfortable. That is what this account of success asks of a life — not a slogan, but a
standing way of choosing.
Notice what the defence does. It does not shout the conclusion louder. It concedes a fair point, locates
the false premise the objection rests on, revises where revision is owed and holds where it is not. That
is what a good defence looks like.


## 7.2. Being your own adversary


With a live opponent, others supply the objections. Alone, you must supply them yourself, and be
as hard on your own thesis as a hostile examiner would be. The following six standard objections
apply to almost any thesis about the good life. Run every one against your claim before you consider
it finished.

Method 7 (the six standard objections).
1. Vague term. Your key word (“flourishing,” “power to act,” “success”) is undefined. Give a
case where it clearly fails to apply, or admit it means whatever you want it to mean. (This is
the conceptualising of Chapter 4, turned on yourself.)
2. Counterexample. Here is a person who has what your thesis prizes and is plainly living
badly (or lacks it and is plainly living well). Your thesis says the opposite.
3. Hidden value. Your thesis quietly assumes a value you have not defended (for example, that
autonomy matters more than belonging). Defend it.
4. Not universal. Does this hold for everyone, or only for people like you? Name whom it fails
for.
5. Self­defeat. By your own criterion, is holding this thesis actually good for you? If not, it
undercuts itself.
6. So what? Once clarified, your claim is either false or trivially true. Which is it?
Pitfall. The most damaging shortcut is the straw man: stating a weak, easily-defeated version
of the counter-argument so that you can knock it down. Graders and peers see it instantly, and
it shows you have not understood the strongest case against yourself. The steel man is required
instead: state the objection in the most forceful form you can, as if you held it, and only then
answer it. A thesis that survives its own strongest objection is worth far more than one that
defeats a feeble one.


## 7.3. Revising, and holding your ground


The point of exposing your thesis to objection is not to abandon it at the first push, nor to cling to it
at any cost. It is to do exactly one of two things, for reasons.
Method 8 (revise or hold).
• Revise when the objection is sound — its premises hold and its inference lands. Concede the
true part and repair the claim so that it accommodates the point. A thesis improved under a
sound objection is stronger, not weaker.
• Hold when the objection is not sound — a false premise, a broken inference, or a conclusion
that overreaches (run the three checks on the objection itself). Then say precisely why it fails,
and keep your claim.
The one thing you may not do is decide in advance. Which way you go must be forced by the
objection, not by your attachment to the thesis.


## 7.4. Drawing the consequences


A thesis defended is not yet a thesis finished. The final move is to ask what your account of success,
now that it has survived objection, actually asks of a life: what it commits you to, what it rules out,
and what it would change in how you choose. A thesis that stops at “here is what success is” has told
you what to believe and not what to do. Drawing the consequences closes that gap — and it is the
step on which the essay ends.

Method 9 (drawing the consequences).
1. Ask what the thesis demands. If this is what success is, what does a life organised around
it require — of your choices, your commitments, what you pursue and what you refuse?
2. Draw only what follows. Each consequence must be entailed by the thesis as defended, not
imported from what you happened to prefer beforehand. Run check 3 of Chapter 3 on your
own consequences: granting the thesis, does this really follow?
3. Include the costly ones. State what the thesis asks that is hard — what it makes you give
up — not only the consequences you find congenial. A list that only flatters the thesis has not
been drawn honestly.
Pitfall. Two failures spoil this step. The first is restating the thesis instead of drawing from it:
“so success is power to act” is the claim again, not a consequence of it. A genuine consequence
names something the thesis does — a choice it forces, a comfort it removes. The second is the
non­sequitur consequence: a practical conclusion that sounds weighty but does not actually
follow from the thesis you defended. Draw what the thesis entails, and nothing more.


## 7.5. Defending under pressure, and composing the essay


The live defence brings back Chapter 3. Under objection you must, in real time, run the three checks
on the attack (is the objector’s premise true? does the inference follow?) and on your own reply. A
good defence is not louder insistence. It is showing exactly where the objection fails, or conceding
the point and repairing the thesis. The worked thesis above did the second: it gave ground on the
true part and came out sharper.
The written essay is this same movement, set down in order: state the claim; put two of the five criteria
into productive tension; raise the strongest counter-argument, steel-manned; answer it, revising or
holding for reasons; and draw the consequence — what your account of success asks of a life.
Rigour is marked, not agreement: premises, inferences, whether the conclusion follows, and how the
objection is handled.
One part of this cannot be rehearsed on the page. The six standard objections are the ones you thought
of, and so the ones you were already braced for; real pressure comes from an objection you did not see
coming, pressed by someone who will push where it hurts. To approximate it, do the final rehearsal
out loud: hand a friend or study partner the six standard objections and your thesis, and have them
fire the objections at you in an order you do not know, following up on your weakest answer. A book
can give you the objections; only a live opponent can supply the surprise.
Exercises
7.1. Here is a claim: “To succeed is to live a life of maximum freedom, with as few obligations
to others as possible.” Using the six standard objections, write the single strongest counter-
argument to it, stated as if you held it (steel-man it, do not straw-man it).
7.2. Take the claim in Exercise (1) together with the objection you wrote. Now answer your own
objection. Your answer must either show where the objection fails — a false premise, a broken
inference, or a conclusion that overreaches — and hold, or concede a fair point and repair the
claim, and revise; and it must say which it is doing and why.

7.3. Put two of the five criteria from Chapter 6 into productive tension over a single goal (for
instance, Seneca versus Mill on chasing recognition). Show what work the disagreement does
that citing either one alone would not.
7.4. Take the sharpened thesis of the worked example — “seek the status that enlarges what you
can do” — and draw its consequences: state two things it asks of a life, one you find congenial
and one you find costly. For each, show that it follows from the thesis (check 3 of Chapter 3)
rather than merely restating it.
7.5. Capstone. Construct your own thesis on what it means to succeed, carrying all five
parts (claim; two thinkers in productive tension; a steel-manned counter-argument; an answer
defended against further objection; and the consequences drawn). Then run all six standard
objections against it and note, for each, whether your thesis survives — and if not, whether you
revise or hold, and why.
Answers. (1) A strong steel-man uses the counterexample and hidden-value objections together: “Maximum freedom
with minimal obligation describes a lonely and shrinking life, not a successful one. The goods that make a life worth
living — friendship, love, meaningful work, raising others — are all built out of obligations freely taken on. Aristotle’s
flourishing needs them, and even Spinoza’s power to act grows through connection, not isolation. The thesis has
quietly assumed that obligation is a cost rather than, often, the very thing that enlarges us.” A weak straw-man to
avoid: “So you want no friends at all?” (2) A good answer revises: concede that chosen obligations can enlarge us, then
repair the claim to prize not “as few obligations as possible” but “obligations you have genuinely chosen over ones
imposed by inertia or others’ expectations.” That keeps a real thesis (success as authorship of your commitments)
while surrendering the indefensible part (obligation as always a cost) — and it says openly that it is revising, because
the objection’s core was sound. (3) For example: Seneca distrusts recognition as the crowd’s approval, which would
not survive his subtraction; Mill notes recognition still adds real well-being and can open doors to more. The tension
forces a condition — pursue recognition only where it converts into goods that survive Seneca’s subtraction — which
neither thinker states alone. (4) A good answer draws consequences that act, not restatements: a congenial one such
as “I can pursue a demanding, capability-building role with a clear conscience, even when it looks less glamorous
than the alternative,” and a costly one such as “I must be ready to turn down the more prestigious, better-paid job
when it would shrink what I can actually do — and give up the reassurance the title would have bought.” Each must
follow from “seek the status that enlarges what you can do”: the test is that dropping the thesis would remove the
consequence, which a mere restatement fails. (5) Self-check: your thesis passes only if (i) the claim could be argued
against, (ii) the two thinkers actually disagree and the disagreement does work, (iii) the objection is one you would
find hard to answer, not a soft target, (iv) for at least one of the six objections you had to either revise the claim or
give a real reason for holding it, and (v) the consequences you draw genuinely follow from the defended thesis and
include at least one that costs you something. A thesis that survived all six untouched, or whose consequences merely
restate it, has almost certainly not been pushed hard enough.
Going to the source (optional). This book deliberately avoids “what did X believe.” But if you
want to read an original after meeting an instrument, one short starting point each: Plato, Euthy-
phro and Laches (Socratic questioning and aporia); Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics (eudaimonia);
Epictetus, Handbook (Enchiridion) (the dichotomy of control); Spinoza, Ethics (the conatus and
the emotions); Seneca, On the Happy Life (De vita beata); Mill, Utilitarianism (what happiness
is). None is required. Each repays the reading only once you already hold the idea as a tool.
