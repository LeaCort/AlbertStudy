Preface
This book is about one question — what does it mean to succeed? — and about learning to think it
through for yourself, instead of living by an answer you took from others without ever inspecting it.
By the end, you will be able to:
• take a settled notion, such as “a successful life”, and turn it into a genuine question — and see what
that question, and your own claims, quietly take for granted;
• state a position and support it with reasons and examples whose steps hold;
• object to a position, your own or someone else’s, and press it until it either holds or breaks down;
• define the notions at stake and tell apart the ones that are easily confused, such as success,
happiness, pleasure and flourishing;
• draw out what a question or a story means when it is not said outright;
• revise a position when a sound objection requires it, and keep it, with reasons, when none does;
• write and defend a reasoned essay that proposes a thesis on success, faces the strongest objection
to it, and draws out what it asks of a life.
None of these is a fact to memorise. Each is a skill of thinking, and you do not yet hold them in the
form this book aims to give you.


## How to use this book


This book teaches skills, not facts, and you cannot acquire a skill by reading about it, any more than
you can learn to swim from a manual. So read actively. The first five chapters build the moves of
the practice one at a time — questioning, arguing, objecting, conceptualising, interpreting. The sixth
gives you five instruments for thinking about success: five thinkers, each offering a criterion for a
good life. The seventh puts everything to work in a thesis of your own, defended against objection.
Two points about the approach will spare you a common misunderstanding of what “philosophy”
means here. First, thinkers enter only as instruments. When we bring one in — Socrates, Aristotle,
Epictetus, Spinoza, Seneca or Mill — the question is never “what did this person believe?” It is always
“what does this let us see that we could not see without it?” A thinker who shows you nothing new
is, for this book, of no use, however famous. Second, the only thing that counts is the rigour of
the reasoning: its premises, its steps, and whether its conclusion really follows. A position earns
nothing by agreeing with a famous name, and loses nothing by contradicting one, as long as the
reasoning holds. A conclusion you dislike, argued well, is worth more than a conclusion you share,
argued badly.
At the end of every chapter there are exercises. Do them before you read the answers: the answers
are there so that you can check your own work, not so that you can skip it. A position is tested only
when someone objects to it, and this book has no classmates. So where a class would press you, the
book gives you a substitute: a set of standard objections you can turn on your own claims, and a
procedure for testing your own position as an opponent would (Chapter 7). A claim you have tested
against objection yourself is worth far more than one you have only accepted.
