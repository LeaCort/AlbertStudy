3. Objecting, and following an idea to
aporia
To object is not to disagree loudly. It is to show, precisely, what does not hold in a claim — your own
or someone else’s — and to push it until it either survives or breaks. This chapter gives you the tools
to object well, and introduces the place a serious objection can lead: aporia.


## 3.1. The three checks


An argument (Figure 2) can fail in exactly three places, so there are exactly three checks. You run
them whether or not you like the conclusion.
Method 3 (the three checks).
1. Are the premises true, or at least defensible? An argument built on a false starting point
proves nothing, however neatly it proceeds.
2. Does each inference follow? Granting the premises, is each step really allowed, or has
something extra been quietly assumed?
3. Does the conclusion follow from the premises taken together? Sometimes every step
looks fine, but a key word has shifted meaning along the way, or the conclusion claims more
than the premises support.
The sharpest single form of objection is the counterexample: one clear case in which the claim’s
own terms are met and yet its conclusion plainly fails. A counterexample does not need a theory. It
needs one undeniable case, and it can bring down a confident generalisation on its own.
Example — a counterexample at work. Claim: “A successful life is a life of high achievement.”
Counterexample: a celebrated surgeon, decorated and productive, who is bitter, friendless and
would undo it all for one honest relationship. If that life can be full of achievement and still fail
to be one we would call successful, then achievement cannot be what success is. The single case
forces the claim to narrow or fall.


## 3.2. The cardinal rule: separate the conclusion from the argument


Pitfall. The most common error in evaluation, and the one to be strictest about, is letting
your agreement or disagreement with the conclusion decide your verdict on the argument. A
conclusion you agree with can rest on a bad argument; a conclusion you find wrong can rest on
an excellent one. When you find yourself calling an argument “good” and notice you only mean
“I agree with where it ends up,” run the three checks again from the start. Objecting well means
being as strict with the arguments you like as with the ones you do not.

## 3.3. Aporia: following an idea to its breaking point


When you press a claim with objections, one of two things happens. Either it survives — repaired,
narrowed, better for the pressure — or it reaches a point where every available answer fails, and the
confident position dissolves. That second outcome has a name.
Definition 5. Aporia (Greek: “no way through”) is the impasse a position reaches when it is
pressed until it can no longer stand as stated: each defence of it either contradicts something
already granted or assumes what was to be shown. Aporia is not the same as being confused. It
is the precise recognition that a position you were sure of does not hold, and why.
Reaching aporia is the engine of Socrates’ method. In Plato’s early dialogues someone offers a
confident definition — of piety, of courage, of justice — Socrates draws out what it commits them
to, produces a case where it fails, the definition is revised, and the revised version fails in turn, until
the interlocutor arrives at I no longer know what I thought I knew. That arrival is not the failure of the
enquiry. It is its first real result.
Hypothesis: success is wealth
counterexample: the rich, wretched heir
Revised: success is wealth used well
objection: “used well” assumes the good
life it was meant to define
Aporia: we do not yet know what success is
Figure 3: The path to aporia. A confident hypothesis is met with a counterexample, revised, and met
again; the revision turns out to assume the very thing it was meant to explain. The end state is not
defeat but a cleared ground: the easy answers are gone, and the real question — what is success? —
now stands exposed. This is the movement Chapter 7 asks you to run against your own thesis.
The value of aporia is that it clears the ground: it removes the false answers. Before it, you had a
confident answer blocking the question. After it, the false answers are gone and the genuine question
is finally in view. Most real philosophical progress begins here: you cannot look for a good answer
while a bad one occupies the space.
Pitfall. Aporia is easy to misread as “so nothing can be known — every view is as good as every
other.” That inverts it. Reaching aporia is a precise result: it shows that these particular answers
fail, and exactly why. It does not show that no answer exists, and it gives no excuse to give up.
The honest response to aporia is to keep working — with the bad answers now ruled out — not
to conclude that working is pointless.
Exercises
3.1. Objecting means locating the failure. For this argument, say which of the three checks it fails
and where: “People who meditate are calmer. Calm people make better decisions. So everyone
should meditate before every decision.”

3.2. Give a counterexample to each claim, and say what the claim must narrow to in order to
survive it: (a) “A successful person is one others admire.” (b) “If you work hard enough, you will
succeed.”
3.3. A friend argues: “Cheating on this exam is fine, because everyone else is doing it, and you
should not put yourself at a disadvantage.” Suppose you strongly disagree with the conclusion.
Object to the argument anyway: which checks does it fail, and where? Note that you reached the
verdict without leaning on your dislike of the conclusion.
3.4. Take the claim “success is being recognised by your peers.” Press it: state it, raise a coun-
terexample, revise it, then raise an objection to the revision. Do you reach a stable position or an
aporia? Show the step where it breaks, if it breaks.
3.5. Take one belief you hold about how to live or work and run the three checks on it yourself,
pushing until it either survives (say why) or reaches an aporia (show the exact step where every
defence fails).
Answers. (1) Mainly check 3: even granting both premises, “should meditate before every decision” overreaches —
the premises support meditating before important decisions at most, and ignore the cost of delaying every trivial one.
Check 1 is also shaky (both premises are broad generalisations). (2) (a) Counterexample: a demagogue widely admired
for terrible reasons — admiration can track the wrong things; narrow to “admired for a life actually worth admiring,”
which pushes the question back onto what that is. (b) Counterexample: people who work extremely hard and fail
through bad luck or circumstance; narrow to “hard work raises the chances of success,” dropping the guarantee. (3)
Check 1: “everyone else is doing it” is probably false and, even if true, irrelevant to whether the act is right; and the
hidden premise “you should never put yourself at a disadvantage” is indefensible as stated. The reasoning fails on
its own terms, independent of the conclusion. (4) Pressing: “recognised by peers” meets a counterexample (someone
recognised for work they know to be hollow); revise to “recognised for genuinely good work”; but now “genuinely
good” is doing all the work and recognition none — the revision assumes the standard of good it was supposed to
supply. That is an aporia: recognition cannot be what success is. (5) Self-check: a good answer either defends the
belief through the three checks or exhibits the precise step at which every repair either contradicts something already
granted or assumes what it was meant to establish.
