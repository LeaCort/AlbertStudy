6. Five criteria for a successful life
The first five chapters built the moves of the practice. Now we put five instruments in your hands:
five thinkers, each offering a criterion for judging whether a life or a desire counts as good. We do
not ask which is “correct.” We ask what each one reveals, and what it is blind to. Each is met as a
short extract and used as a tool, in the spirit of Chapter 5: the question is always “what does this let
us see?”, never “what did this person believe?”


## 6.1. Aristotle: eudaimonia and the whole life


Example — the pleasant evening versus the good life. An evening of easy pleasure — good
food, good company, nothing demanded of you — is plainly enjoyable. Yet a life made of nothing
but such evenings, year after year, strikes most people as somehow wasted, even if it is pleasant
the whole way through. That reaction — that a life can be pleasant and still not good — is exactly
what Aristotle’s criterion is built to explain. It is the pleasure/flourishing distinction of Table 1,
turned into a test.
Framework 1 (Aristotle: eudaimonia). For Aristotle the highest human good is eudaimo­
nia, usually translated as “flourishing” or “living well.” It is not a feeling or a passing state. It is
an activity of the soul across a complete life, lived in accordance with reason and virtue. A
desire or a goal is good to the degree that pursuing it contributes to such a flourishing life, rather
than to passing pleasure or an external reward.
The diagnostic question Aristotle hands you is: does acting on this move me toward a life that is good
taken as a whole? Eudaimonia is the test, and its span — a complete life, not a moment — is what
makes it a tool rather than a slogan. Its blind spot: it says little about where a desire came from, or
how it feels from the inside right now.


## 6.2. Epictetus: the dichotomy of control


Example — the delayed flight. Your flight is cancelled and you will miss an important meeting.
One traveller rages at the gate agent, refreshes the app, and is miserable for hours. Another accepts
within seconds that the cancellation is not hers to undo, and turns to what is hers: rebooking, a
calm message to the meeting, using the time. Same event. The difference is entirely in where each
traveller put their effort.
Framework 2 (Epictetus: the dichotomy of control). Epictetus, a Stoic, starts from the
dichotomy of control. Some things are up to us and some are not up to us. Up to us are our
judgements, our choices, our desires and our responses. Not up to us are external events, our
body, our reputation, our wealth, and the actions of other people. Freedom and calm come from
investing effort and identity only in what is up to us, and accepting the rest.

Figure 4: Epictetus’s dichotomy of control. The Stoic discipline is to keep effort and self-worth inside

yours to command, not on the outer ring that fortune can take back.
The diagnostic move is a sorting one: faced with a goal or a setback, draw the line — what here is
genuinely up to me, and what am I merely suffering as if it were? A success staked on the outer ring
(reputation, others’ approval) is, for Epictetus, never secure. Its blind spot: the line can be misused
as an excuse for passivity, and the framework says little about which of the things up to us are worth
pursuing.
Pitfall. The dichotomy is easy to misread as passivity: “the outcome is not up to me, so I will
do nothing.” That inverts it. What is up to you is the choice and the effort you commit to it.
What is not up to you is the result. Epictetus asks for full effort on the choice and calm about
the result — not withdrawal.


## 6.3. Spinoza: conatus and joy


Example — two promotions. Two people take demanding promotions. A year later, the first is
more capable, more connected, and more able to make things happen — the role has enlarged her.
The second is anxious, dependent on the approval the title brings, and less able to act freely than
before — the role has diminished him, even though the title is impressive. Same external success,
opposite effect on the person.
Framework 3 (Spinoza: conatus and joy). For Spinoza, each thing strives to persevere in its
own being; this striving is the conatus. Joy is the felt increase of your power to act; sadness is
its decrease. A desire or a goal is to be judged by whether acting on it increases your genuine
power to act — and so your joy — or shrinks it.
The diagnostic question here is internal: does acting on this enlarge my power to act, or shrink it?
Unlike Aristotle’s test, it does not appeal to a picture of the whole good life; it asks only what the goal
does to you. In the example, the first promotion increases the power to act (joy), the second decreases

## 6.4. Seneca: the happy life versus the merely successful one


Example — the life that looks good only from outside. Picture a life that looks perfect from
outside: wealth, a famous name, invitations everywhere, the constant approval of the crowd. Now
learn that the person inside it is restless, fearful of losing it all, and unable to say what any of it is
for. The life looks successful from outside and is wretched from within. Seneca’s whole criterion
turns on that gap — between a life that looks successful and one that is good.
Framework 4 (Seneca: the happy life). In On the Happy Life, Seneca argues that the happy
life (vita beata) is one lived in accordance with reason and virtue, self-possessed and free of
the fear of loss — not one measured by wealth, pleasure, or the crowd’s approval. The approval
of the many, he insists, is no evidence of a good life; often the reverse. The test is to strip a life
of fortune and applause and ask what remains that is genuinely its own.
The diagnostic question is subtractive: if I removed the money, the status and the applause, what of this
life would still stand? Whatever survives that subtraction is the part that was really yours; whatever
vanishes was borrowed from fortune and the crowd. This is the sharpest tool the course offers against
the inherited scoreboard, because it targets exactly the external markers the scoreboard is made of.
Its blind spot: pressed too far, “strip away all externals” can sound as if health, security and friends
count for nothing — which even Seneca does not quite hold, and which Aristotle (external goods
matter) directly disputes.
6.5. Mill: happiness as well­being, and the trouble of measuring it
Example — the satisfied fool. Offered a life of simple, reliable contentment — untroubled,
undemanding, mildly pleased throughout — many people hesitate to call it the best life, even
though it scores high on sheer contentment. Something in us ranks a harder, richer, sometimes
discontented life above it. Mill takes that intuition seriously and tries to build it into the account
of happiness itself.
Framework 5 (Mill: utilitarian happiness). For Mill, the good is happiness, understood as
well-being: pleasure and the absence of pain. Not all pleasures count equally: Mill distinguishes
higher pleasures (of intellect, feeling, and character) from lower ones, holding that “it is better
to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied.” What makes the view utilitarian is the standard
it sets: an action, a choice or a life is judged by how much well-being it produces for everyone it
affects, each person’s well­being counting equally — your own no more than anyone else’s.
The good, on this view, is the greatest total well­being, weighted toward the higher pleasures.
The diagnostic question is aggregative and impartial: does this add to well-being — not only mine, but
that of everyone it affects — counting the higher goods for more? Alone among the five criteria, Mill’s
looks beyond the person whose life is in question to its effect on others; a life aimed only at one’s
own well-being already misses his standard. It is also the most measurable in ambition — it points at

Table 2: Five criteria for a successful life as competing instruments. A single case — a visible, high-
status job with little real growth — can pass one test and fail another: Seneca and Spinoza distrust it
(borrowed approval; shrinking power to act), Mill is unsure (hard to measure), while Aristotle stays
open (it might still fit a flourishing life). The disagreement is the information. Chapter 7 asks you to
put two of these into productive tension.
Remark. Seneca’s subtractive question — what survives if fortune is stripped away? — is simply
invisible to Mill, who is summing well-being across everyone affected, including the goods
fortune supplies. Spinoza’s question — what does this do to my power to act? — is invisible to
Aristotle, who takes the desire as given and asks only where it leads. That is why we hold five
instruments rather than one: each sees a dimension the others cannot.
Exercises
6.1. Match each thinker to the one question it puts to a desire: Aristotle, Epictetus, Spinoza,
Seneca, Mill. Questions: (a) does this grow or shrink my power to act? (b) stripped of fortune
and applause, what remains? (c) does this fit a whole life lived well? (d) does this add to the well-
being of everyone affected? (e) is this built on what is up to me?
6.2. A colleague wins a prestigious award voted on by peers, and you find yourself wanting one
too. State briefly what each of the five criteria says about pursuing it.

6.3. Design (or recall) a real case on which the five criteria disagree: at least one approves and at
least one distrusts the same goal. Explain the disagreement, and say what information it gives
you that a single criterion would have hidden.
6.4. Read this one-line extract in the spirit of Chapter 5: Epictetus writes that we are disturbed
“not by things, but by our judgements about things.” State what this lets you see about a recent
setback of your own that the setback itself did not show.
6.5. Take one real ambition of your own that you treat as obviously worth having. Run all five
criteria over it. Be especially honest with Seneca’s subtraction and Spinoza’s question.
Answers. (1) Aristotle → (c); Epictetus → (e); Spinoza → (a); Seneca → (b); Mill → (d). (2) Seneca: an award is the
crowd’s approval, concentrated into a single prize — strip it away and ask what remains; likely little of your own.
Spinoza: does winning it enlarge your power to act, or only your standing in others’ eyes? Epictetus: the vote is not up
to you (it depends on peers), so staking your peace on it is staking it on the outer ring. Aristotle: an award is a passing
external good; it does little for a flourishing life, though it need not be against one. Mill: judged by total well-being,
the award adds a little (recognition is pleasant, may open doors) but touches few people besides you, and the gain
is small and hard to measure. (3) A strong answer names the specific clash — e.g. a demanding public career that
Seneca and Spinoza flag (borrowed approval, shrinking power to act) yet Aristotle could still count toward flourishing
if it builds real virtues — and draws the lesson that “I want it,” “it is good for me,” and “it is really mine” are separate
questions that can come apart. (4) Self-check: a good answer relocates the disturbance from the event (outer ring) to
a judgement you made about it (up to you), and shows what that reframing opens — not a denial that the setback was
real, but a recognition of which part of the pain was yours to revise. (5) Self-check: Seneca’s subtraction should make
you at least slightly uncomfortable; if it did not, you probably ran it on an ambition you had already decided was safe.
