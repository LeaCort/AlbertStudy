4. Conceptualising: defining and
distinguishing
Many disagreements are not disagreements at all — they are two people using the same word for
different things. And many questions cannot even be answered until the key notion is made sharp.
Conceptualising is the skill of defining a notion, and distinguishing it from its neighbours, precisely
enough to think with.


## 4.1. When a word carries the disagreement


Example — two meanings of one word. Two friends argue for an hour about whether a mutual
acquaintance is “successful.” One means well-off and well-known — the other means living a
life worth living. They never disagree about a single fact about the acquaintance’s life. The entire
dispute is carried by the word “successful,” which each is using for a different thing. Until the word
is pinned down, there is nothing real to argue about.
Definition 6. To conceptualise is to define a notion (say what it is and is not), distinguish
it from notions it is easily confused with, and articulate how the related notions fit together.
A good conceptualisation turns a vague word you argue with into a sharp notion you can argue
about.


## 4.2. Defining a notion


A definition is tested by cases. A good one is neither too broad (it lets in things that plainly are not
instances) nor too narrow (it excludes things that plainly are). You sharpen a definition the way you
press a hypothesis in Chapter 3: propose it, find a case it gets wrong, and adjust.
Method 4 (sharpening a notion).
1. Gather clear cases on both sides: things the notion plainly covers, and things it plainly does
not.
2. Propose a mark — a feature that the yes-cases share and the no-cases lack.
3. Test it against the cases. If a no-case has the mark, the definition is too broad; if a yes-case
lacks it, too narrow.
4. Adjust and re­test, until the mark includes every yes-case and excludes every no-case.


## 4.3. Distinguishing the notions of success


The central conceptual work of this course is to pull apart four notions that ordinary talk runs
together. They are not synonyms, and the differences carry the whole argument of the book.

Table 1: Four notions ordinary talk treats as one. Success is external and measured; happiness is
how one’s own life feels from the inside; pleasure is momentary feeling; flourishing is a whole life
actually going well in activity, whether or not it feels that way. Keeping them apart is what lets the
book’s central question — whether a successful life is a good one — even be asked. Chapter 6 supplies
the thinkers who each define one of these more sharply.
Three distinctions do the work. Success versus happiness: one is an external score, the other an
inner state, and they can come apart — the person highest on the scoreboard need not be the one
whose life feels good, or is good. Pleasure versus flourishing: one is a feeling in the moment, the
other a verdict on a life; an evening can be pleasant, and a life made only of such evenings can still
seem wasted. And the subtlest pair, happiness versus flourishing: happiness is how a life feels
from the inside; flourishing is whether the life is actually going well, in activity. The two usually go
together, but they can separate — a person can feel content while their life, judged as a whole, is
going badly, and a demanding life can be genuinely flourishing without feeling happy the whole way
through. That last distinction — feeling versus a life actually going well — is the one Aristotle builds
a whole framework on (Chapter 6).
Pitfall. Before any dispute about the good life, ask whether it is verbal or real. A verbal dispute
is one that would vanish if the two sides fixed their terms (“he is successful” / “he is not” —
where one means wealthy and the other means fulfilled). A real dispute survives the fixing: the
two sides agree on what the words mean and still disagree about the case. Do not spend an hour
on a verbal dispute dressed as a real one. Fix the word first.
Exercises
4.1. A proposed definition: “A successful person is one who has achieved their goals.” Give one
case that shows it too broad and one that shows it too narrow, then repair it.

4.3. Distinguish pleasure from flourishing using a single example in which the two point in

4.4. Two people argue about whether an artist who died poor and unknown but did work she
loved was “successful.” Show how to tell whether their dispute is verbal or real, and what each
would have to say for it to be real.
4.5. Take a notion you use to judge lives (“driven,” “settled,” “free,” …). Sharpen it with the four-
step method: two yes-cases, two no-cases, a mark, and a test that catches at least one case your
first attempt got wrong.
Answers. (1) Too broad: someone whose only goal was to be left alone and achieved it — goal-achievement alone
does not make a life successful if the goals were empty. Too narrow: someone widely regarded as hugely successful
who never set explicit goals at all. Repair: tie success not to any achieved goals but to goals worth achieving — which
pushes the question onto what makes a goal worth it. (2) (a) pleasure. (b) success and, if she values it, flourishing. (c)
success in the thin external sense only, and not even that for long — neither happiness nor flourishing. (d) happiness
and flourishing, with little conventional success. (3) Example: an addict’s high is intense pleasure while the life
around it collapses — maximal pleasure, minimal flourishing. The mark: pleasure is a momentary feeling; flourishing
is a property of the whole life’s activity, which the pleasure is actively undermining. (4) It is verbal if they simply
mean different things by “successful” (external recognition vs a life well spent) — fix the word and the disagreement
dissolves. It is real only if they agree on a meaning — say, “a life that went well as a whole” — and still disagree about
whether the artist’s life qualifies. (5) Self-check: a good answer produces a mark that visibly re-sorts at least one case
the first attempt misclassified, showing the definition actually did some work.
