1. Turning success into a question
Everything in this book starts from one move: taking something you have always treated as an answer
and making it a question. This chapter is about that move, and about the first skill it requires —
asking a question that opens a real problem instead of hiding an opinion.


## 1.1. From a given to a question


Example — the inherited scoreboard. Ask most people what a successful life looks like and
the answers come quickly and ready-made: a high salary, an impressive title, recognition, the right
neighbourhood. Ask why these count as success, and the answers become much weaker. Mostly
they were absorbed — from family, from friends, from social media, from the surrounding culture
— and never inspected. The list is treated as a given: something you measure yourself against, not
something you ask about.
To make something a question is to stop treating it as settled and start asking what it rests on.
“Success is a high salary and an impressive title” is a given. “What makes a life successful, and why
those things rather than others?” is a question. The difference is not cosmetic. A given can only be
met or missed. A question has an inside: it can be opened, and what it assumes can be brought into
the open.
Definition 1. A genuine question opens a problem: it invites you to look for an answer that
is not already decided by the way the question is put. A loaded question hides a claim inside
itself and only pretends to ask — it has already answered, and merely asks you to agree.


## 1.2. What a question takes for granted


The reason questioning is a skill, and not just curiosity, is that every question carries presupposi­
tions: things that must already be taken as true for the question to make sense as asked. Expose
them, and a confident question often turns out to assume exactly what should be examined.
Definition 2. A presupposition of a question or a claim is something it takes for granted —
something that must hold for the question to be askable, or the claim assertable, in the form
given. A presupposition is not stated; it is assumed. The first skill of the examined life is to make
the unstated assumption visible.
A question can take things for granted on several levels at once. It helps to sort them.

Figure 1: The presuppositions buried in a single question. “Why aren’t you more ambitious?” assumes
facts (that you are not very ambitious), an intention read into you (that you were trying to be and
failed), a state of mind (that this reflects a real want of yours), a worldview (that lives rank on a scale
of ambition that matters), and a belief (that more ambition would be better). None of these is stated;
each can be false. Answering the question as asked concedes all five.
The five layers in Figure 1 are worth naming, because a question can be sound on one and rotten on
another:
• Facts: what the question assumes is the case (“you are not ambitious”).
• Intention: what it assumes you were trying to do (“you meant to be, and fell short”).
• State of mind: what it assumes about your inner life (“this is what you actually want”).
• Worldview: the picture of how things are ranked or connected that the question relies on
(“ambition is the axis on which lives are measured”).
• Beliefs: the values it treats as obvious (“more ambition is better”).
Example — surfacing a presupposition. A student says: “I need to decide whether to sell out
and take the corporate job, or stay true to myself.” The phrase “sell out” already answers the
question: it presupposes that the corporate job is a betrayal of the real self, and that “the real self” is
the non-corporate one. Neither is obvious. Surfacing the presupposition — “you are assuming the
corporate path is a betrayal; is it?” — reopens a question the loaded phrasing had quietly closed.


## 1.3. Asking without hiding your opinion in the question


The hardest part of questioning is keeping your own answer out of the question. A question that
carries your opinion cannot open a problem, because it has already shut it.
Method 1 (forming a question that opens a problem).
1. State it as an open question, not a leading one. “Isn’t recognition obviously worth
pursuing?” leads; “What, if anything, makes recognition worth pursuing?” opens.
2. Check the presuppositions. Ask what must be true for the question to make sense as posed,
across the five layers, and make each assumption explicit.
3. Remove the ones that beg the question. If a presupposition is exactly what is in dispute,
the question must not assume it. Rephrase until the answer is not decided in advance.
This is the move Socrates makes throughout Plato’s early dialogues, and it is why we take him as the
model of the method rather than as a source of doctrine. He meets someone who is sure they know
what piety is, or courage, or justice, and he does not argue that they are wrong. He asks: “What is
it?” — and then keeps the question open, refusing every answer that turns out to assume what it was
supposed to explain. The confident interlocutor is not lectured; he is questioned until he sees that

his certainty rested on assumptions he had never inspected. That is the whole method: not to deliver
the answer, but to keep the question genuinely open until the presuppositions surface.
Pitfall. The commonest failure in questioning is the loaded question: presenting a claim as
if it were a question. “Why do people waste their lives chasing money?” is not a question about
money; it is the opinion “chasing money wastes your life” put into the form of a question. It
cannot teach you anything, because any answer you accept has already granted the point. When
you notice you are asking a question you already know the answer to, you are almost certainly
leading, not asking.
Pitfall. A second failure is confusing a question of fact with a question of value. “Do most
graduates earn more in finance?” is a factual question, settled by evidence. “Should you go into
finance to earn more?” is a question of value, and no salary figure settles it. Sliding from the first
to the second — “the data shows finance pays more, so obviously you should” — buries a value
judgement inside a fact. Keep them apart.
Exercises
1.1. For each item, say whether it is a given or a genuine question, and if it is a given, rewrite
it as a genuine question: (a) “A successful career is one that keeps climbing.” (b) “What would
make this career worth having?” (c) “Everyone wants recognition, so recognition must matter.”
(d) “Isn’t it obvious that security beats risk?”
1.2. Take the question “Why don’t you want a more impressive job?” Surface one presupposition
at each of the five layers (facts, intention, state of mind, worldview, beliefs).
1.3. Each question below is loaded. Identify the opinion it hides, then rewrite it as an open
question that does not hide that opinion in it. (a) “Why do ambitious people always end up
lonely?” (b) “How long will you keep settling for an ordinary life?”
1.4. Classify each as a question of fact or of value, and explain: (a) “Do people who change careers
report higher satisfaction?” (b) “Should you change careers if it makes you happier but poorer?”
(c) “Is a life of service better than a life of achievement?”
1.5. Take one thing you currently treat as an obvious mark of a successful life. Write it first as
the given you actually hold, then turn it into a genuine question, and list two presuppositions
your original given was resting on.
Answers. (1) (a) given → “Does a successful career have to keep climbing, or can it succeed by other measures?” (b)
already a genuine question. (c) given (it also hides a bad inference — that what everyone wants must matter) →
“Does the fact that people want recognition show that it matters?” (d) loaded given → “Does security beat risk, and
under what conditions?” (2) For example — facts: that your job is not impressive; intention: that you were aiming
for impressive and missed; state of mind: that impressiveness is something you actually want; worldview: that jobs
are ranked on an “impressiveness” axis that matters; belief: that a more impressive job would be a better one. (3) (a)
hides “ambition causes loneliness” → “What relationship, if any, is there between ambition and loneliness?” (b) hides
“your life is ordinary and that is a failure” → “What would make a life more than ordinary, and is that something
worth pursuing?” (4) (a) fact — settled by data on reported satisfaction. (b) value — no datum settles whether the
trade is worth it. (c) value — “better” here is a judgement, not a measurement. (5) Self-check: a good answer produces
a question whose answer is genuinely not fixed by the wording, and names two assumptions (typically one factual,
one about value) that the original given had quietly relied on.
