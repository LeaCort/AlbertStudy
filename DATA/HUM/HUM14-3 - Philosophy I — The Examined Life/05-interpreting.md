5. Interpreting
Not everything is said outright. A question can mean more than it asks; a story can carry a point it
never states. Interpreting is the skill of drawing out what is not explicit — the intention behind a
question, the point of a text or a tale — and doing so in a way the words actually support.


## 5.1. Reading what is not said


Example — the question behind the question. A colleague asks, apparently idly, “Do you think
it is ever too late to change career?” Taken literally, it is a question about age and feasibility. But the
way it is asked — to you, now, with that weight — suggests the real question is not factual at all. It
is closer to: “Would it be foolish, or forgivable, for me to leave what I have built?” Answering the
literal question (“no, people retrain at fifty all the time”) misses the person entirely. Interpreting
means hearing the question that is actually being asked.
Definition 7. To interpret is to draw out a meaning that is not stated explicitly: the intention
behind a question (what the asker is really after), or the point of a text or story (the claim it
makes without asserting it). An interpretation is not a free invention: it is a claim about meaning,
and like any claim it must be supported — here, by the text and the situation.
Method 5 (interpreting a question or a text).
1. Ask what problem is really in play. What is the asker, or the text, actually wrestling with,
beneath the literal words?
2. Read the signals. Word choice, what is emphasised, what is left out, the situation in which
it is said — these carry the meaning the surface omits.
3. State the point as a claim, one that could be argued for or against.
4. Check it against the text. A good interpretation is supported by what is actually there;
discard readings the text will not bear.


## 5.2. Interpreting a story


A story states nothing and can still make a claim. Reading it is a matter of finding the point it enacts.
Example — the fisherman and the consultant. A management consultant on holiday finds a
fisherman dozing beside his boat, the day’s small catch already sold. “If you fished more hours,”
says the consultant, “you could buy a second boat, then a fleet, sell the company, and get rich.” “And
then?” asks the fisherman. “Then you could relax, sleep in the sun, and fish a little for pleasure.”
“But,” says the fisherman, “that is what I am doing now.”
The tale asserts no thesis. Yet its point is unmistakable once you read for it: the consultant’s whole
ladder of success leads back to the life the fisherman already has, which exposes the ladder as
possibly pointless. But read carefully. The story is not a proof that ambition is worthless — that
would be over-reading it. It makes one sharp point (an inherited idea of success can send you on
a long journey back to where you already were) and leaves open what the consultant might fairly

Pitfall. Interpretation fails in two opposite directions. Under­reading takes everything liter-
ally and misses the point — hearing “is it too late?” as a question about age, or the fisherman tale
as advice about boats. Over­reading imposes your own thesis on the material and calls it the
meaning — announcing that the tale “proves ambition is a disease,” which the story neither says
nor supports. The corrective for both is the same: tie every claim about meaning to something
actually in the words or the situation.
Exercises
5.1. For each, give the literal question and then the likely real question behind it, and name the
signal that tells them apart: (a) a student, the night before results, asks “Does the grade even
matter in the long run?” (b) someone who has just been promoted asks a friend, “Do you think
I have changed?”
5.2. A one-line tale: “A man spent his life building a taller and taller tower so that he could see
further; by the time it was tall enough, he had forgotten what he had wanted to see.” State the
point in one sentence, as a claim, and name the detail in the tale that supports it.
5.3. Give one under-reading and one over-reading of the fisherman tale, and say in each case
what the reading ignores or invents.
5.4. Take a short saying you have heard about success or work (for example, “the obstacle is the
way”). State what it is really claiming, argue for your reading from its words, and note one thing
it leaves unsaid that a critic could press.
Answers. (1) (a) Literal: whether grades predict long-run outcomes. Real: “if I did badly, is it a catastrophe or
survivable?” — the signal is the timing (spoken in fear the night before results), not a genuine interest in longitudinal
data. (b) Literal: whether the friend has noticed a change. Real: “have I become someone I would not like?” — the
signal is that the worry surfaces exactly at the promotion. (2) Point: pursuing a means so single-mindedly can make
you lose the end it was for. Support: the man forgets what he wanted to see — the detail that the goal, not just the
tower, is what he loses. (3) Under-reading: “it is about work-life balance for fishermen” — ignores that the tale is
a parable about ends and means generally. Over-reading: “it proves that all ambition is self-defeating” — invents a
universal thesis the tale does not argue and cannot support (it shows one ladder folding back on itself, not that every
ladder does). (4) Self-check: a good answer states a definite claim, quotes or points to the words that carry it, and
honestly flags what the saying omits (for “the obstacle is the way,” that some obstacles are simply harmful and not
opportunities in disguise).
