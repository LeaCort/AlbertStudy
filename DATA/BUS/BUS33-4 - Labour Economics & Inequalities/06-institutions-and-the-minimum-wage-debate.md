6. Institutions and the minimum-wage
debate
Labour markets are never left bare: minimum wages, unions, and unemployment insurance sit on
top of them in every advanced economy. The competitive model of Chapters 1 and 2 makes a sharp
— and sharply negative — prediction about each. We put that case first, read straight off the supply-
and-demand diagram you already have, and only then ask why every one of these institutions is
adopted regardless.


## 6.1. The competitive case against wage floors and insurance


First, a term the insurance argument leans on.
Definition 17. A worker’s reservation wage is the lowest wage at which they will accept a job
rather than keep searching. Anything that raises the value of remaining unemployed — more
generous benefits, better prospects — raises the reservation wage, and a higher reservation wage
means longer search. (This is the search counterpart of the participation reservation wage of
Chapter 1: there, the wage below which a worker supplies no hours; here, the wage below which
a searcher rejects an offer and keeps looking.)

## 6.2. Why these institutions exist all the same


If the diagram is so damning, why does every rich democracy keep wage floors, recognise unions,
and insure the unemployed? Because the competitive diagram is not the whole model, and because
employment is not the only thing at stake.
Collective bargaining as countervailing power. The competitive case assumes firms are wage-
takers. Where an employer has monopsony power — the case developed in the next section —
a single worker bargains from weakness, and a union (or a wage floor) restores a balance the lone
worker cannot. Read this way, collective bargaining is not a distortion added to a competitive market
but a correction to an uncompetitive one: it can raise the wage and employment over exactly the
range in which a minimum wage can, for the same monopsony reason. Unions also give workers
a voice — a channel to improve conditions and reduce costly turnover — which is why unionised
firms are not uniformly less productive.
The political economy. Insurance against job loss is a good private markets underprovide: a worker
cannot buy a policy against a recession, because the risk hits everyone at once and those most exposed
are the least able to pay. The state steps in where the private market fails. And workers are not only
agents in a model but a political coalition: they vote, and in a democracy the median voter is a
wage-earner who values income security and a wage floor. Labour-market institutions are, in the
end, the outcome of that political economy as much as of any efficiency calculation — which is why
they persist even where the textbook diagram counts them a cost.

## 6.3. The Card–Krueger reframing and monopsony


Example — New Jersey, 1992. On 1 April 1992 New Jersey raised its minimum wage from
$4.25 to $5.05; neighbouring Pennsylvania did not. Card and Krueger surveyed over 400 fast-food
restaurants on both sides of the border, before and after. The competitive model predicted New
Jersey employment should fall relative to Pennsylvania. It did not: measured full-time-equivalent
employment per restaurant rose slightly in New Jersey while falling in Pennsylvania — a differ-
ence of roughly +2.75 workers per restaurant in New Jersey’s favour, statistically indistinguishable
from zero and certainly not the sharp fall predicted. This single well-designed comparison changed
economists’ view of the minimum wage more than years of theoretical argument had.
Why might a minimum wage fail to cut jobs — or even raise them? Because the competitive model
assumes firms are wage-takers. If instead an employer has monopsony power (it is large relative
to its local labour market), it restrains hiring to hold wages down, and a minimum wage can push
both wage and employment up over a range.

Remark. What the natural-experiment evidence changed, in three registers:
• Empirical: modest minimum-wage rises have small or negligible employment effects, contra-
dicting the naïve competitive prediction.
• Theoretical: it revived monopsony (employer wage-setting power) as a serious description
of low-wage labour markets.
• Methodological: it helped install the natural-experiment standard now expected of any
causal claim in the field — the subject of Chapter 9.
