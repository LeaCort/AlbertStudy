Preface
You arrive able to reason about a consumer at a tangency, a firm at MC = MR, and a market that
clears — the microeconomics of your first semester — and about aggregate output, the Phillips curve,
and the policy levers that move a whole economy — the macroeconomics of your second. This course
aims that toolkit at one market in particular, the labour market, and by the end it will let you do a
set of things those two courses did not equip you for. You will be able to:
• model a worker’s labour supply as a work–leisure choice, split a wage change into its income and
substitution effects, and say when the supply curve bends backward;
• derive a firm’s labour demand from the marginal product of labour and predict how employment
responds to a wage rise or a cheaper machine;
• compute the internal rate of return to a degree, and tell the human-capital reading of a wage
premium from the signalling one;
• decompose an observed wage gap into an explained and an unexplained part, and say precisely
what the unexplained part is and is not;
• explain why unemployment persists even with vacancies open, read a Beveridge curve, and
separate frictional, structural, and cyclical unemployment;
• judge what a minimum wage, a union, or unemployment insurance does to employment — using
the best evidence, not the textbook prediction alone;
• measure a distribution with a Lorenz curve, a Gini coefficient, and percentile ratios, follow
Piketty’s 𝑟 > 𝑔 mechanism, read global inequality off the elephant curve, and argue why inequality
carries efficiency costs and not only moral ones;
• and — the core habit this course builds — tell a causal claim from a correlational one, by naming
the counterfactual a study constructs and the assumption that identifies it.
None of this is available to you now; all of it will be by the last page. Monetary figures are written
in euros purely for concreteness; every argument is a statement about ratios and rates that reads the
same in any currency.
