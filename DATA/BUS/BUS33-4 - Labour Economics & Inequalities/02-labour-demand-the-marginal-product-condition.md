2. Labour demand: the marginal-product
condition
The other blade of the market. A firm does not demand labour for its own sake but for what an extra
worker produces: labour demand is a derived demand, derived from the demand for the output
labour makes. This is the firm theory from your microeconomics course, re-read with labour as the
variable input.


## 2.1. Deriving demand from marginal product


Example — the last barista. A café owner asks: should I hire one more barista? The new hire
serves €120 of coffee a day (her marginal product valued at the menu price). She costs €100 in
wages. Hire her — €20 of surplus. The owner keeps hiring until the next barista would add
exactly €100, no more. Because each additional worker in a fixed café adds a little less than the last
(diminishing returns), there is a unique stopping point, and it falls when wages rise.

## 2.2. Capital–labour substitution and the automation shock


How far the firm cuts labour when wages rise depends on whether it can swap in capital. That swap
is governed by one elasticity.
Definition 4. The elasticity of substitution 𝜎 ≥ 0 between capital and labour measures how
the input mix responds to the relative factor price 𝑤/𝑟 (with 𝑟 the rental price of capital). It is the
ratio of the percentage change in the capital–labour ratio 𝐾/𝐿 to the percentage change in
the relative price 𝑤/𝑟 that caused it:

A larger 𝜎 means capital substitutes more readily for labour: when 𝑤/𝑟 rises (a wage rise, or a
labour-saving technology that cheapens effective capital) the firm re-mixes toward capital more
aggressively, which makes its labour demand more wage-elastic. A small 𝜎 means the two are
poor substitutes and the input mix barely responds.
Note carefully what 𝜎 measures: the response of the input ratio 𝐾/𝐿, not of the employment level
𝐿 — a distinction the next example turns on.
Example — reading sigma off a shock. Two plants each face a 10% rise in 𝑤/𝑟 (a labour-saving
machine has cheapened capital). In plant 𝐻, 𝜎 = 1.5: the capital-to-labour ratio 𝐾/𝐿 jumps by 1.5×
10% = 15% — the firm re-mixes sharply toward machines. In plant 𝐿, 𝜎 = 0.3: 𝐾/𝐿 rises only 3%
and the mix barely moves. Read this correctly: 𝜎 pins down the input mix, not the headcount. A
15% rise in 𝐾/𝐿 says capital grows 15% faster than labour — it does not say employment falls 15%
(with capital pouring in, 𝐿 falls by much less than that, and by how much depends on the plant’s
output too). What the comparison does show cleanly is that the plant with the larger 𝜎 re-mixes
away from labour far more readily.

The employment level response has two pieces. A rise in 𝑤/𝑟 makes the firm substitute capital
for labour at a given output (the substitution effect, stronger the larger 𝜎), and — for a wage rise
— it lifts marginal cost, so the firm produces and hires less (the scale effect). The elasticity of
labour demand, the size of the employment fall, therefore rises with 𝜎 but never equals it: 𝜎 times
the percentage change in 𝑤/𝑟 is the percentage change in the ratio 𝐾/𝐿, of which only a fraction
(capital’s cost share) is the substitution-driven fall in 𝐿, with the scale effect added on top. For a
cheaper machine the substitution piece still sheds labour, but the scale piece now runs the other
way — lower costs expand output and can pull hiring back up — which is precisely why automation’s
net effect on employment is ambiguous.
Remark. A labour-saving automation shock enters exactly here. A cheaper, more capable
machine lowers the effective price of capital 𝑟, raising 𝑤/𝑟. If 𝜎 is large the firm substitutes
machines for workers and labour demand contracts; if the technology instead raises MP
𝐿
(machines that make workers more productive), labour demand can rise. Whether automation
destroys or creates jobs is, at root, a question about 𝜎 and about which margin the technology
hits — the theme we return to in Chapter 10.
