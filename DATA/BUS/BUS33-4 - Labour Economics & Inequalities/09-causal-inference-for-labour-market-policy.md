9. Causal inference for labour-market
policy
Every chapter so far has bumped into the same wall: a policy and an outcome move together, but
does the policy cause the outcome? This chapter makes the methods explicit. They are the reason
labour economics looks the way it does today, and they are the most transferable skill in the course.


## 9.1. The counterfactual problem


Example — the fundamental problem. A region raises its training subsidy and unemployment
falls 2 points. Did the subsidy do it? To know, you would compare the region’s actual outcome
with what the same region would have done without the subsidy — but that world is never
observed. The missing counterfactual is the whole difficulty; a raw before/after number silently
assumes nothing else changed (it always does).
Definition 23. The causal effect of a treatment on a unit is the difference between its outcome
with and without treatment — but only one is ever observed. This fundamental problem of
causal inference means a naïve treated-vs-untreated or before-vs-after comparison confounds
the policy with every other difference between the comparison groups or periods. All credible
methods are devices for constructing a believable counterfactual.
One method builds the counterfactual outright; the rest hunt for one in data the world happened
to produce. We take them in that order — from the manufactured counterfactual to the ever-cleverer
ways of finding a ready-made one.


## 9.2. Randomised controlled trials: manufacturing a counterfactual


Example — a training programme by lottery. A city can fund a training place for only half of
its 2 000 applicants. Rather than pick, it draws lots. Winners and losers are then, in expectation,
identical in ability, motivation, and circumstance — the lottery guarantees it — so the losers’
later employment is the winners’ missing counterfactual. Any gap between the two groups is the
programme’s effect, with nothing left to confound it.
Definition 24. A randomised controlled trial (RCT) assigns the treatment at random — by
lottery — so that treated and control groups are, in expectation, identical in every respect but the
treatment. Randomisation builds the missing counterfactual rather than searching for one. It is
therefore the benchmark against which every method below is judged: each is credible exactly
to the degree it approximates random assignment.
Pitfall. The benchmark is not the whole answer, on two counts.
• Feasibility. Many labour-market questions cannot be randomised at all — you cannot assign
a country its minimum wage, or a generation its level of inequality.

• Validity. A clean trial settles internal validity — inside the experiment the effect is real — by
construction. It does not settle external validity — whether the same effect holds at national
scale or in another country. Run on volunteers in one city, a result may not generalise; and
people who know they are studied may behave differently.
This is why the field pairs trials with the observational methods below, each covering the other’s
weakness.


## 9.3. Natural experiments: finding a counterfactual


When randomisation is impossible, the next best thing is a counterfactual the world produced by
accident.
Definition 25. A natural experiment exploits variation in a policy generated by an external
event — a new law, an administrative border, a lottery, a date cutoff — that is plausibly
unrelated to the outcome’s other determinants, so that being treated is “as good as random.”
Card and Krueger’s state border is the canonical case: New Jersey raised its minimum wage
and neighbouring Pennsylvania did not, so a fast-food job on the Pennsylvania side is a credible
counterfactual for one on the New Jersey side. We compute that very comparison, formally, as
difference-in-differences below.
9.4. Instrumental variables and regression discontinuity: building a
clever counterfactual
Sometimes no ready-made control group exists, but a sliver of the treatment is assigned as-good-as-
randomly by something outside the outcome. Two designs turn that sliver into a counterfactual.
Example — quarter of birth — a return to schooling that dodges ability bias. To measure
the return to schooling free of ability bias, Angrist and Krueger needed something that shifts how
long people stay in school but is otherwise unrelated to their ability or family. They found it in an
unlikely place: quarter of birth. Because compulsory-schooling laws tie the legal leaving age to a
birthday, men born early in the calendar year reach that age with slightly less schooling than those
born later — for a reason, the month of birth, seemingly unrelated to earnings. Suppose the first-
quarter-born average 12.7 years of schooling and log-earnings of 4.60, while the later-born average
12.8 years and 4.61. Take the gap in earnings between the two groups and divide by the gap in their
schooling:

The numerator is the instrument’s whole effect on the outcome; dividing by its effect on the

example, 𝑍 is quarter of birth, 𝐷 years of schooling, and 𝑌 log-earnings.
Pitfall. Everything rests on the exclusion restriction — and it is an assumption no data
can confirm. The quarter-of-birth estimate is the causal return only if birth timing reaches
earnings through no channel but schooling. Name the ways it might not: children born in
different seasons start school at different relative ages, which can affect attainment and confi-
dence on their own; and season of birth is mildly correlated with maternal health and family
background, which affect earnings directly. If any such second door exists, the numerator picks
it up too and the ratio no longer identifies the return to schooling. A weak instrument — here
the denominator is a tenth of a year — makes this worse, because it magnifies any leak in the
exclusion restriction. An instrument is only ever as good as the argument that it has no second
path to the outcome.
Example — Maimonides' rule — the effect of class size. An old rule invoked in Israeli schools
caps a class at 40: enrol 40 pupils and they sit in one class of 40; enrol 41 and the grade splits
into two classes of about 20. Average class size therefore plunges each time enrolment crosses a
multiple of 40 — a sharp, mechanical discontinuity with nothing to do with how good or rich a
school is. Angrist and Lavy compare achievement in schools just above a cutoff (small classes,
by the rule) with those just below (large classes): two schools enrolling 39 and 41 are alike in
everything a parent would choose on, yet land in classes of 39 and 21 — as good as a randomly
assigned class size. The jump in achievement at the cutoff is the effect of class size.
Definition 27. A regression-discontinuity (RD) design generalises Maimonides’ rule: it
exploits a threshold in a rule where units just below and just above a cutoff are near-identical,
yet only those past it are treated, so the jump in the outcome at the cutoff — not the overall
slope — is the causal effect (Figure 10).

Example — Card–Krueger as difference-in-differences. New Jersey is the treated group,
Pennsylvania the control. Suppose average full-time-equivalent employment per restaurant moved
as follows:
NJ:20.4 → 21.0 (+0.6), PA:23.3 → 21.2 (−2.1).
Then 𝜏̂= (+0.6)−(−2.1) = +2.7 FTE per restaurant. The before/after change within New Jersey
(+0.6) would have understated things; differencing out Pennsylvania’s common downturn (−2.1)
isolates the policy’s effect — and it is not negative. (These stylised figures reproduce the sign and
rough magnitude of Card and Krueger’s finding.)
Pitfall. A causal claim is only as good as its named identifying assumption. For DiD that is
parallel trends — and it fails if something else hit the two groups differently at the same time, or
if the groups were already diverging beforehand (check pre-trends!). “Treated and control just
differ by 𝑋” is not identification until you have argued the counterfactual is credible. Correlation
plus a fitted line is not a causal effect.
Exercises
9.1. State the fundamental problem of causal inference in one sentence, and say why a raw
before-and-after comparison is not enough.
9.2. A region rolls out a training programme. Average monthly earnings in the treated region
move €1 800 → €2 100; in an untreated control region they move €1 750 → €1 900. (a) Compute
the difference-in-differences estimate. (b) Name its identifying assumption. (c) Give one way
that assumption could fail, and how you would check for it.
9.3. For each design, state the identifying assumption it needs and one threat to it. (a) An RD
study grants a hiring subsidy only to firms with at most 50 employees, and compares firms just
under and just over 50. (b) An IV study uses distance to the nearest college as an instrument for
years of schooling. (c) In one sentence, say why a randomised controlled trial is the benchmark,
and give one reason it may still fail to answer a labour-market policy question.
Answers. (1) A unit’s outcomes with and without treatment can never both be observed, so any raw comparison
confounds the policy with every other difference between the groups or periods.
(2) 𝜏̂=(2100−1800)−(1900−1750)=300−150=+€150 per month. Identifying assumption: parallel trends —

earnings trends.
(3) (a) RD assumes firms just below and just above 50 are otherwise identical, so the jump in the outcome at 50 is the
subsidy’s effect; the threat is manipulation — firms staying at 49 to keep the subsidy, which makes those just below
unlike those just above. (b) IV needs relevance (distance shifts schooling) and exclusion (distance affects earnings
only through schooling); the threat is that living far from a college also proxies for rural labour markets or family
background, a second path to earnings. (c) An RCT is the benchmark because random assignment makes treated and
control identical in expectation, building the counterfactual rather than assuming it; but many policies cannot be
randomised, and a trial’s result may not generalise beyond the sample it ran on (external validity).
