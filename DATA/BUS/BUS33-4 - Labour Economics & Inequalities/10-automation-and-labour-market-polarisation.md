10. Automation and labour-market
polarisation
We close on the open question that worries many students entering the job market: will automation
take the jobs? Here the discipline of the previous chapter matters most, because the topic is full of
confident claims the evidence does not support in either direction.


## 10.1. Routine versus non-routine tasks


Example — two jobs, two fates. A payroll clerk and a home-care nurse. The clerk’s core tasks
are routine: codifiable, rule-based, exactly what software does cheaply — so that occupation has
shrunk. The nurse’s tasks are non-routine: physical dexterity, judgement, and human contact
resist codification — so that occupation has grown. The dividing line that predicts exposure is not
“high-skill versus low-skill” but routine versus non-routine.
Definition 29. The task-based view classifies work by whether its tasks are routine (codi-
fiable, rule-based) or non-routine, and whether they are cognitive or manual. Technology
substitutes most readily for routine tasks (which can be programmed) and complements non-
routine tasks (raising their productivity and the demand for them).


## 10.2. Polarisation and complementarity


Definition 30. Job polarisation is the empirical hollowing-out of middle-skill, routine
occupations relative to high-skill non-routine cognitive jobs and low-skill non-routine
manual jobs — a U-shaped change in employment across the skill distribution, documented
across the United States and Europe since roughly 1980. Where machines complement labour,
automation raises wages and employment in the affected tasks; where they substitute, it dis-
places workers. This complement-versus-substitute split is the elasticity of substitution 𝜎 from
Chapter 2, now applied task by task.
Pitfall. Be scrupulous about what economics can and cannot adjudicate here:
• Can: that routine tasks are the most exposed to automation, and that the past four decades
show measurable polarisation — these are well-identified empirical findings.
• Cannot (yet): the net long-run effect on total employment and wages, because automa-
tion simultaneously destroys tasks and creates new ones, and the pace and reach of future
technology — including AI moving into non-routine cognitive work, historically the least
exposed — are genuinely unknown. A forecast of mass technological unemployment and a
forecast of seamless reabsorption are both beyond what current evidence can settle. Honesty
about that boundary — saying plainly what the evidence cannot yet decide — is itself part of
reasoning like an economist.

## 10.3. Policy responses: active labour-market policies and flexicurity


Whatever the net long-run verdict, displacement is real for the individual worker, and how a country
cushions and redirects it is a policy choice with measurable consequences. Two ideas organise the
modern toolkit.
Example — two ways to help a laid-off worker. A plant closes and a machine operator loses her
job. One country simply mails her an unemployment cheque — a passive transfer that protects her
income while she searches. Another adds a caseworker, pays for a retraining course in a growing
occupation, and subsidises the first months of a new employer’s wage bill — active measures
aimed at getting her back into work, and into a better-matched job. Most systems do both; the
balance between them is the policy lever.
Definition 31. Active labour-market policies (ALMPs) promote employment directly rather
than merely replacing lost income. The main instruments are
• job-search assistance: counselling, matching help, and monitoring of search;
• training and retraining: rebuilding the human capital of Chapter 3 when old skills lose their
market;
• hiring subsidies and direct public job creation.
They stand opposite passive policies — unemployment benefits — which insure income but
neither create nor find jobs. When ALMPs are evaluated with the natural-experiment and
difference-in-differences methods of Chapter 9, job-search assistance and well-targeted private-
sector wage subsidies tend to be the most cost-effective; pure public-job-creation schemes fare
worst; and the returns to training are real but slow, showing up mainly in the medium run.
Definition 32. Flexicurity combines three ingredients a naïve reading of the competitive
model treats as at odds:
1. flexibility — light hiring-and-firing rules, so firms adjust employment freely;
2. security — generous unemployment insurance, so a job loss is cushioned (income security,
not job security);
3. activation — strong ALMPs that move the insured worker quickly back into work.
Denmark is the standard case (the flexicurity row of the comparative table in Chapter 6): low
job protection, high benefits, heavy activation spending. The bet is that security and activation
together let the labour market reallocate workers fast without leaving them exposed.
Pitfall. Recall from Chapter 6 that generous benefits raise the reservation wage and lengthen
search — taken alone they should raise frictional unemployment. The flexicurity claim is not
that this effect vanishes but that activation offsets it: monitoring and job-search assistance
pull the reservation wage and search duration back down. Copy the “security” leg without the
“activation” leg and you get the long-search problem without the cure — the components are a
package, not a menu.

10.1. Using the routine/non-routine task framework, explain job polarisation and the comple-
ment/substitute distinction (relating it to the elasticity of substitution). Then list precisely which
claims about automation’s effect on employment current economics can adjudicate and which
remain genuinely uncertain, and why.
10.2. (a) Distinguish active from passive labour-market policies and give two examples of
each. (b) State the three components of flexicurity and explain why, despite generous benefits
raising the reservation wage, the Danish model need not produce high unemployment. (c) A
government copies flexicurity’s generous benefits but funds no activation. Predict the effect on
search duration and frictional unemployment, and justify it.
Answers. (1) Routine tasks (codifiable) are substituted by machines; non-routine tasks are complemented — a task-
level reading of 𝜎. Polarisation is the U-shaped hollowing of middle-skill routine jobs. Economics can adjudicate that
routine work is most exposed and that measurable polarisation occurred since 1980; it cannot yet settle the net long-
run employment/wage effect, because destruction and creation of tasks run together and future technology (including
AI in cognitive work) is genuinely uncertain.
(2) Active policies promote employment directly (job-search assistance, training, hiring subsidies); passive policies
replace income (unemployment benefits). The three flexicurity components are flexibility (light hire/fire rules),
security (generous benefits), and activation (strong ALMPs); benefits alone raise the reservation wage and lengthen
search, but activation offsets this by monitoring search and speeding re-employment, so the package need not raise
unemployment. Copying the benefits without activation keeps the reservation-wage rise uncured: search duration
and frictional unemployment go up.
