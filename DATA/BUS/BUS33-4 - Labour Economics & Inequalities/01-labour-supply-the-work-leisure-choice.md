1. Labour supply: the work–leisure choice
The supply of labour is not a primitive; it is the output of a choice every worker makes — how much
of a fixed endowment of time to sell. Your microeconomics course taught you to read any choice as
constrained optimisation. Here the good being “bought” with forgone wages is leisure, and its price
is the wage.


## 1.1. The constrained-optimisation set-up


Example — the cost of an afternoon off. You earn €20 an hour. Taking Friday afternoon off
— four hours — is not free: it costs you €80 of forgone pay. That €80 is the price of leisure. If
your wage rose to €40, the same afternoon would cost €160: leisure has become twice as expensive,
even though nothing about the afternoon changed. Every labour-supply decision is a purchase of
leisure at a price equal to the wage.

## 1.2. Income and substitution effects of a wage change


This is the price-change decomposition from your microeconomics course, applied to the price of
leisure — and it produces a result with no analogue in ordinary consumer theory.
Example — why a raise can make you work less. A nurse earning €25/hour is offered €35/hour.
Two pulls act at once. Leisure is now dearer relative to consumption, tempting her to work more
(substitution). But she is also richer at any given hours, and if leisure is a normal good she will want
to consume more of it — to work less (income). Which pull wins is an empirical question, and for
most workers the two roughly cancel or the substitution effect wins slightly, so measured hours
change little when the wage rises. The income effect can nonetheless dominate — the clearest case

Proposition 1. A rise in 𝑤 decomposes into two opposing effects on desired hours:
• Substitution effect: leisure becomes more expensive relative to consumption, raising desired
hours (always positive, for a wage rise).
• Income effect: the higher wage raises real income; if leisure is a normal good this raises
desired leisure, lowering hours.
The net effect is ambiguous. When the income effect dominates, the individual labour-supply
curve is backward-bending.

## 1.3. Participation and the reservation wage


Everything so far is the intensive margin — how many hours to work, taking for granted that the
worker works at all. But the first choice is the extensive margin: whether to work. It turns on a
single threshold wage.

Example — the lowest wage worth accepting. A parent of a young child values an hour at
home highly. Offered €8 an hour they stay home — the wage does not cover what that hour is
worth to them. Offered €18 they take the job. Somewhere between lies the wage at which they
are just indifferent between working and not: their reservation wage. Below it they supply zero
hours; at it they enter the market.
