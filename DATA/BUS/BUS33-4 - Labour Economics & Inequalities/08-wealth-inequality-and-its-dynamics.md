8. Wealth inequality and its dynamics
Income is a flow; wealth is the stock that accumulates from it, and wealth is far more concentrated
than income (top-10% wealth shares of 60–70% are common, against top-10% income shares nearer
30–40%). Piketty’s contribution was to give the long-run dynamics of that concentration a disciplined
framework.

concentration mechanism

This is Piketty’s “second fundamental law.” It is a statement about the size of the capital stock

worth five years of national income); if growth slows to 𝑔 = 1.5%, 𝛽 = 6.7. Slower growth

The second claim is best met through a case before its statement.
Example — two families over a generation. The Capital family owns €1m returning 𝑟 = 5%
a year and reinvests it; after 30 years their fortune is 1m×1.0530 ≈ €4.32m. The Labour family’s
wages grow with the economy at 𝑔 = 1.5%; over the same 30 years their earnings rise only
by a factor 1.01530 ≈ 1.56. The ratio of the two grows by 1.0530/1.01530 = (1.05/1.015) 30 ≈ 2.77:
inherited wealth nearly triples relative to earned income, for no reason other than arithmetic —
𝑟 > 𝑔. Historically Piketty puts 𝑟 around 4–5% and 𝑔 around 1–2% over the long run, with the gap
widening as growth slows.
Proposition 5 (the r > g concentration mechanism). Let 𝑟 be the (after-tax) rate of return
on wealth and 𝑔 the growth rate of average income. A wealth-holder who reinvests sees their
fortune grow at about 𝑟, while incomes grow at 𝑔. When
𝑟 > 𝑔,
large existing fortunes pull away from average incomes, so wealth concentrates at the top
over time — a statement about the distribution of wealth, driven by the gap 𝑟−𝑔, not by the
aggregate ratio 𝛽.
Pitfall. Two traps here. First, do not fuse the two propositions: 𝛽 = 𝑠/𝑔 is about the aggregate
stock and says nothing by itself about top shares; concentration at the top is what 𝑟 > 𝑔 drives.

Second, 𝑟 > 𝑔 is a tendency, not a law of motion that runs untouched. It is offset by the
dispersion of returns, by wars and crises that destroy capital, by taxation, by consumption out of
wealth, and by how estates are split across heirs. Piketty’s claim is that absent such offsets the
drift is toward concentration — not that concentration rises monotonically regardless of policy.


## 8.2. Intergenerational mobility and the Great Gatsby Curve


Definition 22. Intergenerational mobility measures how strongly a child’s economic posi-
tion depends on the parents’. It is summarised by the intergenerational elasticity of earnings
(IGE) — the regression coefficient of child on parent log-earnings. A higher elasticity means a
child’s outcome is more tightly bound to the parents’, i.e. lower mobility. Estimated IGEs run
from about 0.15 in the Nordic countries (high mobility) to roughly 0.4–0.5 in the United States
and United Kingdom (low mobility).

Pitfall. The Great Gatsby Curve is a cross-country correlation, not a proven causal chain.
It is consistent with inequality causing immobility (the rich buy decisive advantages for their
children) but also with common third causes. Stating it as “inequality reduces mobility” without
an identification strategy is exactly the inference error the next chapter exists to discipline.


## 8.3. Is inequality really rising? Two objections


The Ginis and top shares of the last two chapters point one way: within advanced economies,
inequality has risen since 1980. Two objections are routinely raised against reading too much into
that. Both deserve an answer — and answering them sharpens the claim rather than dissolving it.

Objection 1: mobility. A snapshot Gini, the argument runs, overstates lifetime inequality, because
people move between ranks — today’s poor student is tomorrow’s earner. If ranks reshuffle each
generation, a high Gini need not mean a rigid society. The answer is the Great Gatsby Curve
just drawn: empirically, mobility is lowest exactly where inequality is highest. The reshuffle
that would offset a high Gini is weakest in the most unequal places, so inequality and immobility
compound rather than cancel. Mobility does not rescue the optimistic reading; it deepens the
pessimistic one.
Objection 2: the elephant curve. Widen the lens from one country to the whole world and global
inequality between individuals has, on the standard measure, fallen: hundreds of millions in Asia
climbed out of poverty. The evidence is the elephant curve (Figure 9) — real-income growth from
1988 to 2008 plotted against a person’s rank in the global distribution, tracing the silhouette of
an elephant. The answer is to read the whole animal. Its raised body — the global middle class,
overwhelmingly in emerging Asia — did enjoy the fastest growth, and that is the genuine good
news. But its lowered trunk shows the lower-middle class of rich countries nearly stagnant, and its
raised tip shows the global top 1% capturing an outsized share: the top 1% is the main winner of
globalisation. Between-country convergence and within-country divergence run at once — which
is exactly why the within-country inequality this course measures did not go away.

## 8.4. Why inequality matters: efficiency, not only fairness


Whether inequality is unfair is a moral question, and economics does not settle those. But there is a
second, entirely positive question an economist can address: does inequality make the economy less
productive? Past some level, the evidence says it can, through five channels.
Framework 1 (the efficiency costs of inequality).
1. Innovation. Talented children of poor families rarely become inventors — the “lost Ein-
steins”: exposure and opportunity, not raw ability, gate who patents. Steep inequality wastes
inventive talent and lowers the innovation rate.

2. Civic trust. More unequal societies report lower interpersonal and institutional trust. Trust
is the lubricant of exchange; where it erodes, contracting, enforcement, and cooperation all
cost more.
3. Infrastructure. When the affluent secede into private substitutes — private schools, security,
transport — political support for shared public goods weakens, and infrastructure with high
social returns is under-provided.
4. Education. Credit constraints and unequal schooling stop able children of poor families from
investing in their own human capital, so the economy’s stock of skill is misallocated — a
direct productivity loss.
5. Safety. Higher inequality is associated with more crime, diverting real resources into protec-
tion and enforcement that produce nothing.
None of this says all inequality is inefficient — some wage spread is the very incentive that draws
effort and investment. The claim is narrower and firmer: past some level, inequality stops paying
for itself in incentives and starts costing output through these five channels. That is why “inequality
matters” is not only a value judgement.
