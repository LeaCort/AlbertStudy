10. The integrated investment case
Everything converges here. The three decisions — invest, finance, return — are not taken in isola-
tion but together, for one company, using the accounting-adjusted inputs you already know how
to build (normalised earnings, free cash flow, ROIC). This is the deliverable the whole sequence has
been building toward, and the foundation a full company valuation later assumes you can produce.
Method 3 (The integrated case). For a single company, assemble the three decisions on one
consistent set of assumptions:
1. Invest. Build the project or strategy as dated, incremental, after-tax free cash flows and
evaluate it with NPV at the appropriate discount rate, cross-checked with IRR (Chapter 2,
Chapter 3).
2. Finance. Choose a financing mix; compute its WACC (Chapter 6); assess how the proposed
leverage moves the cost of capital and where it sits against the firm’s optimal structure
(Chapter 7). Feed this WACC back as the discount rate in step 1.
3. Return. Justify a payout policy for the cash not absorbed by positive-NPV investment,
consistent with the firm’s growth, cash-flow stability, and shareholder base (Chapter 8).
The case is coherent only if the same inputs, risk assumptions, and tax rate run through all three
— the WACC that discounts the project must be the WACC implied by the financing mix, and
the cash distributed must be what is left after funding every positive-NPV project.

Example — Lumio's integrated case, where the decisions collide. Read at low stakes, the
three decisions agree. The €100 m production line has NPV +€8 m at the 7.8% WACC and an IRR
of 11.5% — invest, accept. Lumio is mildly under-leveraged at 𝐷/𝑉 ≈ 37%, so a little new debt
would capture more tax shield and nudge WACC down, strengthening that NPV — finance
helps invest. And the surplus left over funds the steady dividend its income-oriented base values —
return follows cleanly. If the story stopped here it would teach nothing: every decision is obvious.
Now make the stakes real. This year Lumio’s whole slate of positive-NPV projects — the line plus
others — needs about €200 m, but internal free cash flow is only €70 m, and its shareholders rely
on a €30 m dividend. The three decisions now pull against one another, and no choice satisfies
all three:
• Invest wants the full €200 m — every project clears the hurdle.

Integrating is resolving this, not stacking three yes-votes. A defensible answer ranks the slate by
NPV and funds the top projects with about €80 m of new debt — holding net-debt-to-EBITDA near
the ≈ 2.5× optimum (net debt €300 m) and interest coverage above 3× — defers the marginal
projects to next year rather than buying WACC-destroying leverage, and holds the dividend flat
(neither cut nor grown) for a year. Something gives: a few positive-NPV euros wait. That is the
point — the trade-off is unavoidable, and the skill is pricing which decision to bend.


## 10.1. Critiquing a capital allocation proposal


A finished case is not a verdict to be trusted but an argument to be stress­tested. The most valuable
skill in corporate finance is knowing where a model is weakest — because a positive NPV is only
as good as the assumptions underneath it.
Framework 1 (A disciplined critique). Interrogate any capital-allocation proposal along
three axes:
1. Which assumptions drive the NPV? Identify the few inputs the conclusion is most sensi-
tive to — typically the growth rate, margins, terminal value, and discount rate — by
varying each and watching the NPV move (sensitivity and scenario analysis). A project whose
positive NPV survives only at the optimistic end of one assumption is fragile.
2. Where is the cost of capital weakest? The WACC rests on judgment at every input — beta,
the equity risk premium, the target weights, the credit spread (Chapter 4, Chapter 5,
Chapter 6). A small change in any can flip the accept/reject decision, so the estimate’s soft
spots must be named.
3. What can the model not capture? Discounted cash flow ignores real options (the value
of waiting, expanding, or abandoning), strategic interactions (competitor responses), and
risks not expressible as a discount-rate bump. These live outside the spreadsheet and must be
argued in words.
Example — stress­testing Lumio's production line. The line’s +€8 m NPV is modest — about
8% of the €100 m outlay, and a similar fraction of the €108 m of discounted inflows. Two things
can erase it. The cost of capital: the project breaks even exactly at its 11.5% IRR, so if the true
WACC is higher than the 7.8% estimate — the input resting on the most judgment (beta, the equity
risk premium) — the cushion shrinks, vanishing at 11.5%. The cash flows: a uniform shortfall
of about 8% across the four inflows also takes the NPV to zero. The honest recommendation is
therefore not “NPV is €8 m, accept,” but “the line creates value provided the cost of capital stays
below its 11.5% IRR and the cash flows land within about 8% of forecast — and here is what each
assumption is worth.”

Pitfall. A recommendation is defended not by its point estimate but by knowing which
inputs it depends on and how far it survives their plausible range. Beware the false precision
of an NPV quoted to the nearest euro on top of a 6% guessed equity risk premium and a 2%
terminal growth rate chosen with no real basis — the apparent rigour of the arithmetic hides
the fragility of the assumptions. And beware advocacy modelling — a model built to justify a
decision already made: a sponsor who wants the project will find a growth rate that makes NPV
positive. The critic’s job is to surface that assumption and price it.
