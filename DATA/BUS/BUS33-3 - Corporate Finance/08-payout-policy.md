8. Payout policy
The third decision — return — arises once a firm generates cash beyond its profitable investment
needs. How much should go back to shareholders, and through which mechanism? The prior
question is always retention vs distribution.
Example — Lumio's surplus has to go somewhere. Say Lumio throws off €70 m of cash this
year, but its positive-NPV projects — the only ones earning above the cost of capital — absorb just
€50 m. The remaining €20 m has no home inside the firm that beats the WACC. Retaining it would
earn shareholders the WACC at best, which they could earn themselves by investing elsewhere; so
the €20 m should go back to them. That residual is what payout policy is about.
Principle 2 (The retention­vs­distribution rule). Cash should be retained and reinvested
only in projects that earn more than the cost of capital — i.e. positive-NPV projects, equivalently
those with ROIC > WACC. Cash with no such home should be distributed: retaining it to earn
the WACC (or less) inside the firm creates no value the shareholder could not create alone.
Payout policy is what to do with the residual after all positive-NPV investment is funded.
This connects the three decisions into one logic: invest claims every positive-NPV euro; finance sets
the WACC that defines “positive”; return distributes the rest. A firm distributing cash while passing
up positive-NPV projects, or holding idle cash with no projects to fund, has its policy backwards.
Definition 9 (Payout mechanisms).
• A dividend is a cash distribution per share, typically paid on a regular schedule.
• A share buyback (repurchase) returns cash by the firm buying its own shares in the market,
reducing the share count — so each remaining share owns a larger slice of the firm.
In a frictionless MM world the choice is irrelevant: a shareholder wanting cash can sell shares to
make a “homemade dividend,” and a euro paid out is a euro the share price drops.
Example — dividend or buyback: identical in a frictionless world. A firm’s equity is worth
€500 m, in 50 m shares — €10.00 a share — and it will return €50 m of surplus cash. Compare the
two mechanisms for a holder of 100 shares (worth €1,000 today):
Pay €50 m dividend Buy back €50 m
Cash returned €1.00 per share buys 5 m shares
Shares remaining 50 m 45 m
Value per share after (500−50)/50 = €9.00 (500−50)/45 = €10.00
Holder’s 100 shares €900 + €100 cash €1,000 (sells none)
Holder’s total wealth €1,000 €1,000
Either way the holder ends with €1,000. Under the buyback, a holder who wants cash sells 10
shares for €100 and keeps 90 worth €900 — a “homemade dividend” matching what the dividend
would have paid. The euro paid out is exactly the euro the per-share value (dividend) or the share
count (buyback) adjusts by; nothing is created or destroyed. And EPS? If the firm earns €40 m,

EPS rises from 40/50 = €0.80 to 40/45 ≈ €0.89 after the buyback — up 11% — though each share
is worth exactly what it was. That gap between higher EPS and unchanged value is the trap the
Pitfall below returns to.
The choice starts to matter once two frictions enter:
• Taxes. Dividends and the capital gains realised through buybacks are often taxed differently, and
at different times — buybacks defer the tax until the investor sells, dividends tax on receipt. Which
mechanism is tax-favoured depends on jurisdiction and shareholder type.
• Signalling. Because cutting an established dividend is painful and punished by the market,
raising the regular dividend is a credible signal of confidence in durable future cash flows —
managers will not commit unless they believe it. Buybacks are more flexible and discretionary,
so they signal less commitment but suit lumpy or uncertain surplus cash; they also avoid locking
the firm into a payout it may later have to cut.
Example — two firms, two right answers. A mature, cash-rich Lumio with stable cash flows
and few high-return projects suits a steady, growing dividend: it can credibly commit, and
its income-oriented shareholders value the regularity. A young, volatile growth firm with lumpy
surplus cash and a tax-sensitive, capital-gains-oriented shareholder base suits buybacks: flexible,
tax-deferring, and not a promise it might have to break. Same surplus, opposite mechanisms —
driven by growth stage, cash-flow stability, and the shareholder base.
Pitfall. Buybacks do not mechanically “create value” by raising earnings per share. EPS rises
only because the share count falls; if the shares are repurchased above their intrinsic value, the
buyback destroys value for continuing shareholders even as EPS ticks up. Judge a buyback like
any investment — by price versus value, not by its effect on EPS. And a dividend funded by new
borrowing rather than free cash flow is a warning sign, not a sign of health.
Exercises
8.1. In two or three sentences, contrast dividends and buybacks on (a) tax timing and (b) the
signal each sends.
8.2. Recommend a payout mechanism for each, justified by signalling and tax rather than a
payout-ratio rule of thumb: (a) a mature, cash-rich firm with stable flows and income-oriented
shareholders; (b) a young firm with volatile, lumpy surplus cash and tax-sensitive shareholders.
8.3. A CEO argues a buyback “creates value because it raises EPS.” State the flaw and the correct
test for whether a buyback is worthwhile.
Answers. (1) Dividends are taxed on receipt; the capital gain from a buyback is deferred until the investor sells. Raising
a regular dividend is a strong, hard-to-reverse signal of confidence; a buyback is flexible and signals less commitment.
(2) (a) A steady, modestly growing dividend: the firm can credibly commit and its shareholders value regularity. (b)
Buybacks: flexible, tax-deferring, and not a promise that lumpy cash might force it to break.
(3) EPS rises only because the share count falls. If shares are bought above intrinsic value the buyback destroys value
for continuing shareholders despite higher EPS. Judge it by price versus value, like any investment.
