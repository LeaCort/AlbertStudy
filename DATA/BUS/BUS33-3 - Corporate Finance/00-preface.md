Preface
Before this course you learned to read and diagnose a company: to treat the three financial
statements as constructed documents, to compute the ratios and working capital that summarise a
firm’s health, and to isolate operating performance with ROIC and the free cash flow that valuation
discounts. You also met the central test of value creation — a firm creates value only when ROIC >
WACC — but left the WACC, the cost of capital, as a black box. This course opens that box.
Here you stop describing companies and start deciding for them. The subject organises around
Aswath Damodaran’s three corporate-finance decisions:
• Invest — where to deploy capital. Which projects earn more than they cost?
• Finance — how to fund it. What mix of debt and equity, and at what cost?
• Return — how much to give back. Dividends, buybacks, or reinvestment?
Every tool here serves one of the three. Time value of money, NPV and IRR answer invest. The cost
of equity, the cost of debt and WACC answer finance and supply the discount rate that invest needs.
Capital structure and payout policy complete finance and return. And the value-creation metrics —
ROIC against WACC, and EVA — deliver the verdict on whether the three decisions, taken together,
made the firm worth more.
We keep one running company throughout, Lumio, a mid-size lighting manufacturer, so that every
new tool refers back to one set of numbers rather than a fresh example each time (Table 1). Lumio’s
operations earn a return on invested capital of about 10.5% — by the end of the book you will know
the WACC that number must beat, and what to do with the surplus it creates.
