9. Value-creation metrics
We can now close the loop left open earlier. A firm creates value only when it earns more on its
invested capital than that capital costs — and we finally have both halves of the comparison: ROIC
(which you already know how to compute) and WACC (Chapter 6).
Example — Lumio's verdict. Lumio earns ROIC = 10.5% on its €570 m of invested capital, while
that capital costs WACC ≈ 7.8%. Each euro invested therefore earns a 10.5%−7.8% = 2.7% surplus
over its cost, so the firm creates about
2.7%×570 ≈ €15m of value a year.
Equivalently, Lumio’s €60 m of after-tax operating profit (NOPAT) is charged 7.8%×570 ≈ €44.5
m for the capital that produced it, leaving 60−44.5 ≈ €15.5 m of genuine economic value (the two
routes agree up to rounding). A firm reporting the same €45 m net income but earning ROIC <
WACC would be destroying value despite its positive bottom line — the distinction accounting
profit cannot make.
The comparison and the currency figure it implies each have a name.
Definition 10 (The ROIC­vs­WACC test). With ROIC = NOPAT/invested capital and
WACC the cost of capital (Chapter 6), the firm
• creates value when ROIC > WACC,
• is value­neutral when ROIC = WACC,
• destroys value when ROIC < WACC.
The gap ROIC−WACC is the economic spread.
Definition 11 (Economic Value Added). Economic Value Added (EVA) expresses the same
test as a currency amount per period:
EVA = (ROIC−WACC)×invested capital = NOPAT−WACC×invested capital.
The term WACC×invested capital is the capital charge — the euro cost of all the capital tied
up.
Positive EVA is value created over the period; negative EVA is value destroyed, however healthy the
accounting profit looks.
spread = 2.7% ⟹ EVA = +€15 m
0% WACC = 7.8% ROIC = 10.5%
Figure 8: Value creation is a comparison, not a level. Lumio’s 10.5% ROIC clears its 7.8% WACC,
so each euro of the €570 m invested capital earns a 2.7% surplus — about €15 m of EVA a year. The
same ROIC against a 12% WACC would destroy value despite looking healthy in isolation.

EVA charges the firm for all capital employed — including equity, which accounting profit treats as
free — at its true cost. This is precisely why a company can report positive net income yet negative
EVA: net income subtracts the cost of debt (interest) but never the cost of equity. Computing ROIC
and EVA turns the cost of capital from Chapter 4, Chapter 5 and Chapter 6 into a verdict on whether
the company was worth operating.
Pitfall. EVA inherits every measurement choice in ROIC and WACC. Use NOPAT (operating
profit after tax, before interest), not net income, and keep non-operating cash out of invested
capital. On the WACC side, a small error in beta or the ERP can swing the capital charge by
millions and flip the EVA sign — so a borderline EVA verdict should be reported as a range, not
a false-precision point estimate.
Exercises
9.1. A firm has NOPAT €90 m, invested capital €750 m and a WACC of 9%. Compute its ROIC
and its EVA, and state whether it creates or destroys value.
9.2. Another firm reports the same €90 m NOPAT but has invested capital of €1,200 m at the same
9% WACC. Compute its EVA. What does the comparison show about reading net income alone?
9.3. Return to the first firm. If a re-estimate of beta pushes WACC from 9% to 11%, recompute
EVA. What does this say about reporting a borderline EVA as a point estimate?
Answers. (1) ROIC=90/750=12%; spread =12%−9%=3%; EVA=3%×750=€22.5 m (or 90−0.09×750).
Value created.
(2) EVA=90−0.09×1200=−€18 m. Same accounting profit, opposite verdict: net income ignores the cost of the
equity capital tied up, which EVA charges.
(3) EVA=90−0.11×750=€7.5 m — still positive but a third of before. A two-point WACC change swings the
verdict, so a borderline EVA should be reported as a range, not a false-precision point.
