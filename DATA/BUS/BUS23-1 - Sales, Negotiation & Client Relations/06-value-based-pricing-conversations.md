6. Value-Based Pricing Conversations


## 6.1. From the price to the price conversation


Marketing Fundamentals taught value-based pricing: set the number to the value the customer
perceives, not to your cost. This chapter is about the conversation in which that value is claimed
across the table — where anchoring, concession design, and packaging decide how much of the value
you actually capture. The strategy sets the target; the conversation determines the realisation.


## 6.2. Anchoring the conversation


Put €60,000 on the table first and the haggling orbits €60,000; let the buyer open at €30,000 and it
orbits €30,000 — the same service, a different centre of gravity, fixed by whoever named a number
first. The opening figure quietly sets the range everything after it moves within.
Definition 20 (Anchoring in a price conversation). Anchoring is opening with a reference
number that shapes the counterpart’s expectations for the whole negotiation, because of the
anchoring bias (Chapter 2): the final figure is pulled toward the first one stated. A well-chosen
opening anchor — ambitious but justifiable — pulls the settlement in your favour; a timid one
caps your upside before the conversation begins.
Example — The cost of a weak anchor. Two consultants price the same project, worth roughly
€200k of value to the client. Consultant A, afraid of scaring the client, opens at €25k and settles at
€22k. Consultant B opens at €60k — high, but well within the value it creates and defensible by it
— and settles at €42k. Same work, same client; B captured nearly twice as much, almost entirely
because of where each started the conversation. The anchor did most of the work.
Pitfall. Anchor on value, not on cost, and make the anchor defensible. An anchor with no
justification (“€60k because we feel like it”) collapses the moment it is challenged and damages
credibility. An anchor tied to the value the client stands to gain (“€60k against the €200k this will
save you”) holds under pressure. And beware the buyer’s anchor: when they open low, do not
negotiate down from their number — re-anchor against your own.


## 6.3. Concession design


You will rarely hold your anchor exactly; you will concede toward a settlement. Drop from €60k to
€52k to €48k to €46k — shrinking steps — and the buyer senses a floor approaching. Drop €60k,
€52k, €44k, €36k — even steps — and they learn that pushing always wins another chunk, so they
keep pushing. How you concede signals as loudly as the numbers themselves.
Definition 21 (Concession design). Concession design is the planned sequence and size of
your price moves. Concessions should diminish in size as you approach your floor, signalling
that room is running out, and ideally be traded (given only in exchange for something) rather

Example — Trading concessions. Instead of simply dropping price, tie each concession to a
return: “I can come down to €52k if we move to a 2-year term,” then “€48k if you pay quarterly
in advance,” then “I can do €46k, but that’s the floor — and only with the public reference.” Each
concession is smaller than the last (signalling the floor) and buys something (term, cash flow,
marketing), so price drops are recovered elsewhere. This is integrative bargaining (Chapter 1)
inside the price conversation.


## 6.4. Package vs line-item


Quote “everything included, one price” and the buyer cannot pick your offer apart — but cannot see
the value packed inside it either. Itemise every component and you can show that value and quietly
drop the one line they object to — but you invite a grind over each remaining line. One offer, two
framings, opposite risks.
Definition 22 (Package vs line-item pricing). Package pricing quotes one bundled number
for the whole offer; line-item pricing itemises each component with its own price. The trade-
off: packaging obscures component costs and resists itemised discounting (the buyer cannot
pick at individual lines), but reduces transparency and can hide value the buyer would have
paid for; line-item pricing is transparent and lets you show value, but invites component-by-
component negotiation (“we don’t need this line, take it off”).
Example — Choosing the framing. Selling an implementation project: a package “€90k,
everything included” prevents the buyer from stripping out the (high-margin) training and support
lines and stops a line-by-line grind. But if the buyer’s main objection is “we already have our
own trainers,” line-item framing lets you remove that line visibly, preserve the rest, and show
exactly what they are paying for. The right choice depends on whether you most fear unbundling

Pitfall. There is no universally “better” framing — stating the trade-off is the point. Defaulting
to line-item with a buyer who loves to haggle invites a slow erosion of the price, one line at a
time; defaulting to a package with a buyer who needs to justify each line internally can kill the
deal on opacity. Choose deliberately, and be ready to switch framing if the objection reveals the
other pressure.
Structuring a value-based pricing conversation for a named account thus means three deliberate
choices: a defensible value anchor, a concession path that diminishes and trades, and a package-
vs-line-item framing — each chosen with its trade-off stated aloud.
Exercises
6.1. What the buyer knows. You run a mid-size retailer. A consultancy has pitched a project
you privately estimate will save you around €200,000 a year. You intend to push hard on price.
What the seller knows. Your floor for the project is €40,000, and you can flex on term length,
payment timing, and being named as a public reference.
Task. Write the opening anchor, justify it by value (not cost), and design a three-step concession
path in which the steps diminish and each is traded for something.
Debrief. Why should the last concession be flagged as the floor, and what signal would an
increasing concession path send the buyer?
6.2. What the buyer knows. You already employ your own trainers, so you resent paying for
training you will not use — but you cannot judge the rest of the offer line by line.
What the seller knows. Training and support are your highest-margin lines; the buyer loves
to haggle item by item.
Task. Present the same offer once as a package and once as line items. State which buyer
objection each framing handles better and the trade-off each framing accepts.
Debrief. Given this particular buyer, which framing would you actually open with, and why?
6.3. What the buyer knows. You open with an aggressive lowball — €18,000 for the same
€200,000-value project — hoping to drag the settlement down.
What the seller knows. €18,000 is below your floor and detached from any value basis.
Task. Describe concretely how the seller avoids being dragged down by the lowball and re-
anchors the conversation.
Debrief. Why is negotiating down from the buyer’s number a mistake even when you hold
firm on your floor?
Answers. (1) No single key — check that the anchor is justified by the value created for the client (not by cost), that the
three concessions shrink in size, and that each one buys something (a longer term, earlier cash, a reference). The
last step should be flagged as the floor. (2) The package framing best handles unbundling and line-by-line haggling
(the buyer cannot strip out individual lines) at the cost of transparency; line-item best handles a “we don’t need line
X” objection and lets you demonstrate value, at the cost of inviting a component-by-component grind. A complete
answer names both trade-offs, not just which framing wins. (3) Do not counter from their number. Name it as an
opening tactic, mentally discard it, restate the value basis, and re-anchor with your own prepared, value-justified
figure — or decline to counter at all until the extreme is withdrawn (“that’s not a serious basis; here’s where the value
actually sits”).

Chapter takeaways. Value-based pricing sets the target; the price conversation realises it.
Open with a defensible value anchor (and re-anchor against the buyer’s lowball rather than
negotiating down from it). Design concessions to diminish and to be traded, never given freely.
Choose package framing to resist unbundling or line-item to demonstrate value and flex on
components — and state the trade-off of whichever you pick.
