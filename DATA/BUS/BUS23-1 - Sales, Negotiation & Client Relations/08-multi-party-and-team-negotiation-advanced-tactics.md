## 8.1. When the table gets crowded


Everything so far assumed two parties. But put three firms around a table to share one warehouse,
where any two of them could proceed without the third, and the deal is no longer a single negotiation
— it is a contest over who allies with whom, a dimension that does not exist when only two sit
down. Real high-stakes deals — alliances, procurement, M&A, multi-party tenders — involve more
than two parties, or teams on each side, and the extra parties do not just scale the difficulty; they
change its kind, introducing coalitions, internal coordination, and a richer toolkit of tactics and
countermoves.
Definition 26 (Multi-party and team negotiation). Multi-party negotiation involves
three or more parties, which introduces coalitions (subsets that align to swing the outcome)
and shifting majorities — so managing who allies with whom can matter more than any
single bilateral exchange. Team negotiation involves several negotiators on one side, requiring
internal coordination: agreed roles, a single position, and discipline so the other side cannot
split the team.
Pitfall. The signature danger of team negotiation is being divided: a skilled counterpart ad-
dresses the most pliable member, exploits a visible disagreement, or plays your team’s members
against each other. Before the table, agree roles (who leads, who takes notes, who watches the
room), a single position, and a rule that concessions come only from the lead — never improvised
by an individual member under pressure.
Case study — A three-way supply alliance. Three firms are negotiating to jointly fund and
share one warehouse. Sharing saves €900k a year in total; the fight is over how to split that saving
and the running costs. Each firm brings something different, and any two can proceed without
the third by covering the shortfall — so every pair is a possible coalition.
• NovaParts — large and cash-rich; wants control and a long, stable term. Its contribution (cash)
is fungible — anyone’s money is as good as anyone’s.
• Bergen — mid-size; owns the best-located site to host the warehouse; wants a low upfront
outlay.
• Klar — small; brings the logistics software that runs the site (worth about €200k a year of the
total saving); wants a short exit clause and flexibility.
The naïve reading ranks power by size, putting Klar last. But coalitions turn on complementary
contributions, not size. If NovaParts and Bergen ally (cash + site) and try to exclude Klar, they
lose Klar’s software — €200k of the very saving they are dividing. Klar’s walk-away is therefore
stronger than its size suggests: its contribution is hard to replace, while NovaParts’ cash is not. The
lesson of the crowded table: map each party’s BATNA and which contributions are complements
before assuming the biggest player holds the most leverage.

## 8.2. Advanced tactical moves — and their countermoves


“This price is only good until Friday.” “My colleague is the hard one — I’m on your side here.” “We’ll
start at half your number and see.” Each of these is a move designed to work on you, and each has
a calm, specific answer. The following tactics are adversarial: a counterpart will use them on you,
so you must recognise them and, where appropriate, deploy and counter them — and for each, the
countermove is more important than the tactic.
Definition 27 (Three advanced tactics and their countermoves).
• Artificial deadline — a fabricated time limit to force a hasty decision (“this price is only good
until Friday”). Countermove: test whether the deadline is real (“what happens if we decide
next week?”); a manufactured deadline rarely survives a calm question.
• Good cop / bad cop — one negotiator is aggressive, another conciliatory, so you gratefully
concede to the “nice” one. Countermove: name the routine openly (“you two seem to have
different views — sort that out and come back to me”), which dissolves its effect.
• Aggressive anchoring — an extreme opening figure meant to drag the settlement toward
it. Countermove: refuse to treat it as a starting point; re-anchor against your own prepared,
value-based number, or decline to counter until the extreme is withdrawn (“that’s not a serious
basis for discussion — here’s where value actually sits”).
Tactic used on you Countermove
Artificial deadline → test if it's real
Good cop / bad cop → name the routine
Aggressive anchoring → re-anchor on value
Figure 12: Each advanced tactic has a standard countermove. Recognising the tactic is half the
defence; the other half is the calm, specific response that neutralises it.
Example — Countering an artificial deadline. A supplier says, “We can only hold this 20%
discount if you sign today.” Rather than panic-signing, you test it: “We’re interested, but we can’t
make a six-figure decision by tonight. If the discount truly expires, we understand — we’ll proceed
at the standard price next week.” Nine times in ten the deadline softens (“well, let me see what I
can do”), revealing it was a pressure tactic, not a real constraint. The countermove cost nothing
but composure.
Pitfall. Two ethical limits on these tactics. First, several of them (fabricated deadlines, aggres-
sive anchoring detached from any value) edge toward manipulation — the subject of the next
chapter; deploy them knowing the line. Second, the goal is rarely to “win” a single exchange at
the cost of the relationship. In any negotiation with a counterpart you will deal with again, win-
at-all-costs tactics buy a one-time gain and a lasting enemy.

## 8.3. The debrief


The day after a hard negotiation, “it went well” teaches you nothing you can reuse. “We conceded
exclusivity to lock in the two-year term, and we signed under a deadline we never once tested” teaches
you exactly what to repeat and what to fix. A multi-party negotiation is especially hard to learn from
in the moment — too much happens at once, under pressure — so what turns the raw experience
into transferable skill is the debrief afterwards.
Definition 28 (The post-hoc debrief). A debrief is a structured after-action review of a nego-
tiation that names, specifically: what was conceded, why each concession was made, which
tactics were used and countered, where value was created versus claimed, and what you would
do differently. Its purpose is to convert tacit experience into explicit, transferable judgment.
Example — A debrief entry. “We conceded the exclusivity clause (worth €15k/year to us) to
secure the 2-year term we wanted, because our BATNA — a smaller annual deal — was weak and
the term protected our capacity planning. We failed to test their ‘board meeting Friday’ deadline
and signed under time pressure we should have questioned. Next time: probe the deadline, and
lead with an integrative trade on exclusivity rather than giving it away to break the deadlock.”
Specific, honest, and transferable — that is the standard.
Exercises
8.1. What each party knows. Three agencies can share one media-buying desk, saving €600k
a year between them. Big Co brings the cash, Mid Co brings the client relationships, Small Co
brings the only trading software the desk can run. Any two can proceed by covering the third’s
shortfall.
What you know as Small Co. Your software is hard to replace; everyone assumes size decides
power.
Task. Identify a coalition of two that could form, explain how it changes the outcome for the
excluded third party, and say what that third party could do about it.
Debrief. When does a coalition of two actually extract surplus from the third — and when does
excluding the third destroy the value the coalition needs?
8.2. What the counterpart knows. You are a supplier who likes to use pressure tactics: a “sign
by Friday” deadline, a good-cop/bad-cop pairing, and an extreme opening figure.
What you (the buyer) know. You recognise all three as tactics and have prepared your own
value-based number.
Task. For each of the three tactics, write a short script in which the counterpart deploys it and
you apply the countermove, naming the countermove principle each time.
Debrief. Why does naming a tactic openly tend to neutralise it?
8.3. What you know. You have just finished a negotiation (real or simulated) in which you
conceded something, faced at least one tactic, and both created and claimed value.
What the other side knows. Only their side of it — the debrief is for you.
Task. Write a debrief that names what was conceded and why, which tactics appeared, where
value was created versus claimed, and one concrete improvement for next time.
Debrief. What makes a debrief transferable rather than just “it went well”?

8.4. What each party knows. Take the three-way supply alliance case study above: NovaParts
(cash), Bergen (the site), Klar (the software worth €200k of the €900k saving). Each party’s
private wants are in the case study.
Task. With two other people (or by writing all three parts yourself), assign the NovaParts,
Bergen, and Klar briefs and negotiate a split of the €900k saving and the running costs.
Debrief. Which coalition formed, why, and what did the excluded party do about it?
Answers. (1) and (4) have no single key, but a strong answer shows the coalition of two extracting surplus from
the third only when the two together can replace what the third contributes; when the excluded party holds a
complement (as Klar holds the software), excluding it destroys value the coalition needs, so its walk-away is strong
and it can demand a larger share. The excluded party’s best moves: strengthen its BATNA, make its contribution
visibly hard to replace, or split the would-be coalition by offering one member a better bilateral deal. (2) Each script
should show the tactic, then the calm, named countermove: artificial deadline → test whether it is real (“what
happens if we decide next week?”); good cop / bad cop → name the routine openly (“you two seem to disagree
— sort that out and come back to me”); aggressive anchoring → refuse it as a starting point and re-anchor
on your own value-based figure. Check that the countermove principle is named correctly for each. (3) No single
key — a strong debrief is specific: it names each concession and the reason for it, the tactics used and faced, where
value was created versus claimed, and one concrete change for next time. A vague “it went well” reflection fails the
standard, which is honesty and transferability.
Chapter takeaways. More than two parties introduces coalitions and shifting majorities; teams
require internal coordination and discipline against being divided. Advanced tactics — artificial
deadlines, good cop / bad cop, aggressive anchoring — each have a calm countermove (test it,
name it, re-anchor), and the countermove matters more than the tactic. Many tactics edge toward
manipulation and most relationships outlast a single deal, so weigh the long game. Close every
negotiation with a debrief that names what was conceded and why.
