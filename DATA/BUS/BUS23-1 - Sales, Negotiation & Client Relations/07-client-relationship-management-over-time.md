## 7.1. The deal is the beginning, not the end


Everything so far has aimed at closing. But in most businesses the first sale is the least profitable
transaction you will ever have with a client: acquisition is expensive, while renewals, expansion, and
referrals from an existing happy client are cheap. The economics of Marketing Fundamentals — a
brand and a customer base as compounding assets — apply directly to the individual account. This
chapter covers managing a client relationship over its life, in four elements.


## 7.2. The four elements of a CRM plan


One client signs, hears nothing for eleven months, then gets a renewal invoice — and leaves. Another
is guided to a first win within a week, checked in on each month, and offered more only when their
usage shows they need it — and renews for years. Same product; the difference is entirely in how the
relationship was managed, across four elements.
Definition 23 (Client relationship management over time). Managing a client relation-
ship covers four elements:
• Onboarding — bringing a new client to their first real value as quickly and smoothly as
possible.
• Follow-up cadence — the regular, deliberate rhythm of contact after the sale (not only when
something is wrong or you want to sell more).
• Upsell triggers — the observable signals that indicate a genuine opportunity to expand the
account.
• Conflict-resolution protocol — the defined procedure for handling disputes before they end
the relationship.

## 7.3. Onboarding: time-to-first-value


Example — Why onboarding decides retention. A SaaS company finds that clients who reach
their “first successful report” within seven days renew at 90%, while those who take over a month
renew at 40%. The product is identical; the difference is onboarding. The fix is to engineer the
path to first value — a guided setup, a check-in call on day three, a template that produces a win
immediately — because the early experience, not the contract, sets the trajectory of the whole
relationship.


## 7.4. Follow-up cadence


Pitfall. The commonest relationship failure is the only-when-selling contact pattern: the
client hears from you at signing, then silence, then a renewal email asking for money. By then
there is no relationship to renew. A deliberate cadence — scheduled check-ins, value reviews,
useful touches that ask for nothing — maintains the trust that renewals and upsells depend on.
Cadence is regular by design, not triggered only by your needs.


## 7.5. Upsell triggers


A client emails to say they are opening a second office and doubling their team. That is the moment
to propose more seats — it answers a need they have just announced. Pitch the same seats “because
it’s renewal season,” with no such signal, and it is only pressure.
Definition 24 (Upsell triggers). Upsell triggers are observable signals that the client has
a genuine new need you can serve — hitting a usage limit, hiring into a team that would use
more seats, launching in a new region, repeatedly requesting a feature in a higher tier. A trigger-
based upsell serves a real, surfaced need; an untriggered one (“it’s renewal season, let’s push the
premium tier”) is just pressure and erodes trust.
Example — A triggered upsell. A client’s usage dashboard shows they have hit 95% of their
plan’s capacity three months running, and they have twice asked support about a feature in your
enterprise tier. That is the moment — and the justification — to propose the upgrade: it solves a
problem they are visibly already feeling. The trigger makes the upsell a service, not a sales push.


## 7.6. Conflict-resolution protocol


A billing error double-charges a key client. With a protocol in place, the account manager already
knows who the client calls, how fast to acknowledge it, and that she can refund on the spot —
so a tense moment becomes a demonstration of reliability. Without one, the client waits days for
“someone to look into it” and starts shopping around.
Definition 25 (Conflict-resolution protocol). A conflict-resolution protocol is a pre-
agreed procedure for handling disputes — a missed deliverable, a billing error, an outage: who

the client contacts, how fast you acknowledge, who has authority to make it right, and how
the resolution is confirmed. Defined before a conflict arises, it turns a relationship-threatening

Example — A conflict handled well. A service outage costs a key client a day’s work. With
a protocol in place: the account manager acknowledges within the hour (not after legal review),
apologises plainly, states what happened and what is being done, and applies a pre-authorised
remedy (service credit) without a fight. Handled this way, the outage can strengthen the relation-
ship — the client learns they can rely on you when things go wrong. Handled defensively, the same
outage ends it.
A relationship-management plan specifies all four elements for a named account: exactly how the
client is onboarded, at what cadence they are contacted, which observable triggers will prompt an
upsell, and the steps of the conflict protocol — concrete enough to act on, not generic intentions.
Exercises
7.1. What the client knows. You have just signed a €40,000/year contract for an analytics tool.
You are busy, mildly anxious you overbought, and will judge the tool by whether it produces
something useful in the first weeks.
What the account manager knows. Clients who reach first value fast renew far better; you
control onboarding, cadence, upsell timing, and how conflicts are handled.
Task. Write a one-page CRM plan for this named account covering all four elements: a concrete
onboarding path to a first value (with a timeframe), a specific follow-up cadence, at least three
observable upsell triggers, and a step-by-step conflict-resolution protocol.
Debrief. Which single element most protects the renewal, and why?
7.2. What the client knows. (Two clients of the same product.) One reached a first successful
report within seven days; the other took over a month. Both have the same features available.
What the vendor knows. The fast client renews at 90%, the slow one at 40%.
Task. Explain why “time to first value” predicts retention better than the product’s features do,
using this evidence.
Debrief. If features do not explain the gap, what does — and what does that imply about where
to spend effort?
7.3. What the client knows. You have quietly hit 95% of your plan’s capacity three months
running and twice asked support about an enterprise-tier feature.
What the account manager knows. Renewal season is also approaching, and there is pressure
to push the premium tier to everyone.
Task. Distinguish a triggered upsell from an untriggered one with a concrete example of each
for this client, and explain why the difference matters for long-term trust.
Debrief. What observable signal turns a “push” into a “service” here?

Chapter takeaways. The first sale is the start of the profitable relationship, not the end. Manage
it across four elements: onboarding to fast first value (which predicts retention), a deliberate
follow-up cadence (not only-when- selling), upsell triggers tied to observable real needs (not
renewal-season pressure), and a conflict-resolution protocol defined before trouble strikes. A
plan names all four concretely for a specific account.
