3. The Structured Sales Cycle
3.1. From “always be closing” to “always be diagnosing”
The folklore of selling is the smooth talker who charms a reluctant buyer into signing. But watch
two reps sell the same database tool: the first walks the buyer through every feature in the opening
meeting and loses; the second spends that meeting asking how the buyer’s team loses time today,
names a cost the buyer had never added up, and wins. Decades of evidence on complex, high-value
B2B sales say the second rep is the rule, not the exception: in big deals, success turns far more on
the quality of the seller’s questions — their diagnosis of the buyer’s problem — than on the polish
of the close. The philosophy behind this shift has a name.
Definition 8 (Consultative selling). Consultative selling casts the seller as a diagnostician
of the buyer’s problem rather than a promoter of a product. The seller’s first job is to understand
the buyer’s situation and create customer value — helping the buyer see a real problem,
and its cost, more clearly than before — and only then to position the offer as its solution. It
is the opposite of product-led selling, which pushes features before the need is understood. A
consultative seller earns the right to propose by first having genuinely understood.
Everything in this chapter operationalises that stance. We build the structured B2B cycle in two
stages: qualify the opportunity (BANT), then run the discovery (SPIN) — both of which are disci-
plines of understanding before proposing.


## 3.2. BANT: deciding whether to invest at all


An enthusiastic junior manager keeps booking demos of your platform — but has no budget this
year, cannot sign, and won’t decide before “maybe next year.” Every hour you pour into her is an
hour stolen from a buyer who could actually close. Selling effort is finite, so deciding early, and on
evidence rather than enthusiasm, whether a prospect is worth pursuing is its own discipline.
Definition 9 (BANT). BANT qualifies an opportunity on four criteria:
• Budget — can the prospect afford it, and is money allocated?
• Authority — is your contact the decision-maker, or who is?
• Need — is there a real, acknowledged problem your offer solves?
• Timeline — is there a date by which they intend to decide or act?
An opportunity weak on several criteria is not yet worth advancing, and qualifying it out is a
legitimate, valuable outcome.
Example — Qualifying a prospect with BANT. An enthusiastic junior manager wants demo
after demo of your analytics platform. Running BANT: Budget — none allocated this year.
Authority — she cannot sign; her VP can. Need — real, but not urgent. Timeline — “maybe next
year.” The deal is poorly qualified. Rather than pour weeks of demos into it, the disciplined move
is to invest lightly, build the relationship, and ask to be introduced to the VP — while spending
your real selling time on opportunities that pass BANT now.

Pitfall. Happy ears — hearing enthusiasm and mistaking it for a deal — is the classic failure.
An eager contact with no budget, no authority, and no timeline is not a hot lead; they are a time
sink. Qualify honestly and early, and be willing to walk away from a friendly prospect who will
never buy.


## 3.3. Where BANT runs out: complex B2B


BANT is a fast screen, and in a simple sale it is enough. But in a complex B2B deal — several
stakeholders, a long cycle, a formal purchasing process — an opportunity can pass all four BANT
criteria and still die. Budget, a contact, a need, and a timeline tell you the deal could happen; they
do not tell you how it will actually get decided. Three questions probe that.
Framework 2 (Stress-testing a qualified opportunity). Beyond BANT, an apparently qual-
ified complex deal must survive three further tests:
• Decision process — how does this organisation actually buy? Who else signs off, what stages
(legal, security, procurement) does it pass through, and how long does each take? A deal with
no mapped process stalls in stages you never saw coming.
• Decision criteria — what explicit criteria will they judge the options against, and do those
criteria favour you or a competitor? If the criteria are written around a rival’s strengths, the
deal is lost before the demo.
• Internal champion — is there someone inside the account who argues for you when you
are not in the room? Without a champion, your case is only as strong as your last meeting.
Pitfall. A deal that looks qualified on BANT can be quietly doomed: the budget is real but
controlled by a finance committee you have never met (decision process); the need is real but
the written criteria were drafted by a competitor’s advocate (decision criteria); the contact is
enthusiastic but silent internally (no champion). Stress-test every complex opportunity against
these three before you forecast it as winnable.
Remark. More detailed qualification frameworks exist for exactly these gaps — the best known
is MEDDIC, used by many complex-B2B sales teams to formalise metrics, the economic buyer,
decision criteria, decision process, pain, and champion. We do not teach it here; the point to
carry is only that BANT is a screen, not a guarantee, and that a complex deal needs the deeper
questions above.


## 3.4. SPIN: the four question types


Once an opportunity is qualified, the heart of the B2B sale is discovery. Ask a clinic manager “how
do you book patients today?” and you learn they use a paper diary; ask “what does a no-show cost
you?” and she works out it is €40k a year. The second question did what the first could not — it turned
a tolerated nuisance into an urgent, quantified problem, in the buyer’s own words. Neil Rackham,
from analysing thousands of real sales calls, found four question types that do this in sequence,
carrying a buyer from a vague, latent need to an explicit, urgent one.

Definition 10 (SPIN). SPIN structures discovery through four question types, used in rough
sequence:
• Situation — establish the facts of the buyer’s current context (“How do you handle X today?
How many people are involved?”).
• Problem — surface difficulties and dissatisfactions (“Where does that process break down?
What frustrates you about it?”).
• Implication — develop the consequences and cost of those problems (“What does that delay
cost you per month? How does it affect your other teams?”).
• Need-payoff — have the buyer articulate the value of solving it (“If that were fixed, what
would it be worth to you?”).
The power is in the order: implication and need-payoff questions convert a problem the buyer
had learned to live with into one they urgently want solved — and let the buyer, not the seller,
voice the value.

Implication the cost of those difficulties

statement of value (Need-payoff). Implication and need-payoff questions do the heavy lifting: they

Example — A SPIN sequence in miniature. Selling a scheduling tool to a clinic manager:
• S: “How do you book patients today?” — “Phone and a paper diary.”
• P: “Does anything go wrong with that?” — “We double-book sometimes, and no-shows are
high.”
• I: “What does a no-show cost you?” — “About €80 of idle clinician time, maybe ten a week.”
(The buyer has just computed €800/week — €40k/year — out loud.)
• N: “If you could halve no-shows with automated reminders, what would that be worth?” —
“€20k a year, easily, plus less stress.”
Notice the seller never claimed a number. The buyer quantified the problem and voiced the payoff
— far more convincing than any pitch.
The question types are only half the discipline; the quality of each question decides whether
discovery works. This is what a post-hoc critique of a sales call should judge — and it matters more
than how smoothly you closed.
Framework 3 (The marks of a good discovery question). A good discovery question:
• sounds natural, like genuine curiosity, not a line read from a script;

• builds on the buyer’s own previous answer, reusing their words rather than resetting to a
fixed list;
• does not ask what research would have answered — never spend the buyer’s time on
facts you could have looked up (“so, what does your company do?”);
• does not lead the buyer toward your product — “wouldn’t it help to have X?” is a pitch in
disguise, not discovery.
And it respects the order: establish reality first (Situation, Problem), then impact (Implication),
then value (Need-payoff). Ask for value before the buyer feels the impact and the payoff rings
hollow.
Pitfall. The novice rushes through Situation and Problem to get to the pitch, skipping Impli-
cation entirely. That is the costliest omission in B2B selling: without implication questions, the
buyer never feels the cost of inaction, the need stays latent, and the deal stalls at “interesting, let
me think about it.” A SPIN call is judged on the depth of discovery — Situation, Problem, and
above all Implication — not on closing style.
Remark. Note how SPIN operationalises the previous chapters. Implication questions make
the loss of inaction vivid (loss aversion); need-payoff has the buyer make a small verbal com-
mitment to the value (consistency). And the whole method is really interest-discovery from
Chapter 1 — Situation and Problem questions surface the buyer’s true interests beneath their
initial position.


## 3.5. AIDA for B2C


SPIN assumes an identifiable buyer, a complex problem, and time to talk. Much B2C selling has none
of these — a shopper thumbing past an ad, a customer in a checkout queue, a one-to-many message
with no dialogue at all. Think of a poster that stops you with a question, holds you with one relevant
line, makes you want the thing, and tells you exactly how to buy it, all before your bus arrives. For
that world the classic model is AIDA, which describes not the questions to ask but the stages of
attention a message must move a buyer through.
Definition 11 (AIDA). AIDA models a buyer’s progression through four mental stages that a
seller’s message must move them along:
• Attention — get noticed at all amid the clutter.
• Interest — engage them enough to keep paying attention.
• Desire — make them want the product.
• Action — prompt the concrete step (buy, sign up, click) now.
It is a one-directional funnel of attention, suited to shorter, lower-stakes purchases and one-
to-many communication.
The two middle stages are the ones beginners blur, and keeping them apart is the whole craft of a B2C
message. Interest is cognitive: the buyer registers that the message is relevant to them and keeps
paying attention. Desire is motivational: the buyer now wants the thing. You can hold someone’s

interest and still leave them wanting nothing — so the move from Interest to Desire has to be built
deliberately.
Framework 4 (Building Desire: feature → benefit → outcome). Desire is manufactured
by climbing a ladder, not by listing specifications:
• a feature is what the product has (“a memory-foam layer”);
• a benefit is what that feature does for the buyer (“even pressure relief”);
• a customer outcome is the changed situation the buyer ends up in (“you wake up rested
and stop dreading mornings”).
Interest can rest on a feature; Desire lives at the outcome rung. Two further moves deepen it:
proof and risk reduction — testimonials, ratings, guarantees, and free trials that lower the
buyer’s fear of choosing wrong — make the buyer want the product more. And Action is carried
by a clear, low-friction call to action: one obvious next step with the effort stripped out. A vague
or costly ask (“contact us to learn more”) loses buyers who had already reached Desire.
Example — AIDA in a single advertisement. A direct-to-consumer mattress ad. “Still waking
up tired?” grabs Attention. A few seconds on how poor sleep wrecks your focus and mood holds
Interest — it is clearly about the viewer. Then Desire is built up the ladder: not “12 cm of memory
foam” (feature) but “pressure spread evenly so you stop tossing” (benefit) rising to “wake up rested,
sharp by 9 a.m.” (outcome) — reinforced by proof and risk reduction: “23,000 five-star reviews,
100-night trial, full refund if you don’t sleep better.” Finally Action: “Order tonight — one click,
free returns, ships tomorrow,” the next step made frictionless. No discovery, no dialogue — the
message alone carries the buyer through all four stages in thirty seconds.


## 3.6. When are SPIN and AIDA interchangeable?


A frequent confusion is to treat these as rival templates and ask which is “better.” Try SPIN’s patient
questioning on a customer grabbing a phone charger and they walk before your second question;
run AIDA’s “create desire, drive action” push on a €2M deal with a six-person committee and you
pressure people who have not yet understood their own problem. Each model has a home, and the
honest answer is about boundary conditions.

Definition 12 (Boundary conditions). SPIN fits complex, considered, high-value sales with
an identifiable buyer and a problem that can be developed through dialogue. AIDA fits short,
lower-consideration, often one-to-many purchases where there is no discovery conversation.
They become interchangeable only when the buying context collapses the difference — a
single buyer, a simple offer, a short cycle (a small in-store sale, say). They are not interchangeable
where multi-stakeholder discovery is required: AIDA has no mechanism for it, and SPIN is
impossible without a dialogue.
Pitfall. Importing the wrong model is a real and common error. Run a pure AIDA “create desire,
drive action” push on a €2M enterprise deal with a six-person buying committee and you will
pressure a buyer who has not yet understood their own problem — the deal stalls. Conversely,
attempt patient SPIN discovery on an impulse retail purchase and the customer walks before
question two. Match the model to the buying context, not to habit.
Remark. This mirrors B2B-vs-B2C from Marketing Fundamentals: the strategic doctrine is
invariant but its texture changes with the buyer. SPIN and AIDA are that texture made opera-
tional — the same goal (move a buyer to a yes) executed differently depending on whether you
face one deliberating buyer or a crowd of fast ones.
Exercises
3.1. What the buyer knows. You are an ops lead at a 400-person firm. There is budget “in
principle,” you are keen, but any purchase over €50k goes through a finance committee and
a security review, and your CFO ultimately signs. A rival vendor’s champion helped draft the
evaluation checklist last year.
What the seller knows. You sell a €90k analytics platform. Your enthusiastic contact is the ops
lead above.
Task. Qualify this opportunity against all four BANT criteria, then stress-test it on decision
process, decision criteria, and internal champion. State whether you would advance it and what
you would do next.
Debrief. BANT looks green here. Which of the three deeper tests is the real threat, and why can
an opportunity pass BANT and still be unwinnable?
3.2. What the buyer knows. (A clinic manager.) You book patients with a paper diary, double-
book “sometimes,” and have high no-shows, but you have never added up what any of it costs
and you assume “that’s just how clinics run.”
What the seller knows. You sell a scheduling tool with automated reminders.
Task. Script a short SPIN sequence — two questions of each type (Situation, Problem, Impli-
cation, Need-payoff) — in which the Implication questions push the buyer to quantify the cost
and the Need-payoff questions make the buyer state the value.
Debrief. Critique your own script on the four marks of a good discovery question and on the
reality → impact → value order — not on how you would close.
3.3. What the buyer knows. You are a tired commuter scrolling your phone at night; you are
not shopping for anything in particular.

What the seller knows. You are marketing a B2C product of your choice through a single short

Task. Write the ad so it visibly moves through all four AIDA stages. Label each line, and show

Debrief. Which line does the Interest-to-Desire work, and how would the ad read if it stopped

3.4. What the buyer knows. Case A: you are one shopper buying a €30 kettle in a shop. Case
B: you are a six-person committee buying a €2M enterprise system.
What the seller knows. The same seller must choose SPIN or AIDA for each.
Task. State, for each case, whether SPIN and AIDA are interchangeable and name the boundary
condition that decides it. Then explain concretely what goes wrong if a pure AIDA “create desire,
drive action” push is run on Case B.
Debrief. What single feature of the buying context flips the answer between the two cases?
Answers. (1) BANT: Budget in principle but not confirmed; Authority — your contact is not the signer, the CFO is;
Need — real; Timeline — gated by committee and security review. Even read generously, the deeper tests expose
the danger: the decision process (finance committee + security review + CFO sign-off) is long and unmapped, and
above all the decision criteria were shaped by a rival’s champion, while you have no internal champion of your
own. The real threat is the criteria/champion pair: advance only by getting to the CFO, mapping the process, and
recruiting a champion — otherwise the deal is lost before the demo. An opportunity passes BANT yet loses because
BANT is a screen for whether a deal could exist, not for how it gets decided. (2) Check eight questions, two per type;
the two Implication questions must drive toward a quantified cost (e.g. “what does one no-show cost in idle clinician
time? how many a week?”), and the Need-payoff questions must ask the buyer to state the value (“if you halved no-
shows, what would that be worth?”). A sound self-critique judges the questions on the four marks (natural, builds on
the last answer, not answerable by research, not leading to the product) and on the reality → impact → value order
— not on closing polish. (3) Check four labelled lines: Attention grabs notice, Interest holds it (relevance), Desire
climbs feature → benefit → outcome with a proof/risk-reduction element, Action gives one low-friction step. Stopping
at features leaves Interest without Desire — nobody wants a specification. (4) Case A (single buyer, simple low-
stakes offer, short cycle): interchangeable — the context collapses the difference. Case B: not — multi-stakeholder
discovery is required, which AIDA cannot do and SPIN needs a dialogue for. The deciding boundary condition is the
presence of a diagnostic dialogue over a complex, considered purchase. A pure AIDA push on Case B pressures a
committee that has not yet understood its own problem: no Implication questions, so the need stays latent, the deal
stalls at “interesting, let me think about it,” and no one-to-many message reaches every stakeholder.
Chapter takeaways. Qualify before you invest: BANT (Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline)
screens opportunities, and qualifying out is a win — but in complex B2B, stress-test an appar-
ently qualified deal on decision process, decision criteria, and internal champion (BANT is only
a screen, not a guarantee). Run discovery with SPIN — Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-
payoff — whose engine is the implication and need-payoff questions; judge a call on discovery
quality (natural, builds on the last answer, not research or a pitch; reality → impact → value),
not on closing flash. AIDA — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action — is the one-to-many funnel
for short B2C purchases: keep Interest (cognitive) apart from Desire (motivational), build Desire
up the feature → benefit → outcome ladder with proof and a low-friction call to action. SPIN
and AIDA are interchangeable only where the context collapses (single buyer, simple offer, short
cycle) and not where multi-stakeholder discovery is required.
