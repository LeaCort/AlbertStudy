2. The Psychology of Influence
2.1. Why structure isn’t enough
The last chapter gave you the architecture of a deal. But people are not spreadsheets: the same
offer, framed two ways, lands differently; the same price, anchored high or low, feels expensive or
cheap. Influence is the study of the predictable, non-rational ways human judgment moves — and
a negotiator who ignores it will be out-played by one who does not, even with an identical BATNA.
2.2. Cialdini’s six principles
A software buyer stalls for weeks — until the rep mentions that four other firms in her sector adopted
the tool last quarter. She signs within the week. Nothing about the product changed; she simply
saw that comparable others had chosen it, and that tipped her. That pull — following the crowd
under uncertainty — is one of six that Robert Cialdini, after years studying compliance professionals,
distilled from how people are moved to say yes. Each is a reliable shortcut the human mind takes, and
each can serve a buyer’s real interest or exploit it; we meet the honest use here and the exploitative
twin in Chapter 9.
Definition 6 (Cialdini's six principles of influence).
1. Reciprocity — people feel obliged to repay what they receive. A genuine concession, a useful
sample, or real help creates a pull to give back.
2. Commitment and consistency — people act in line with what they have already said or
done, especially publicly. Small initial agreements pave the way to larger ones.
3. Social proof — people look to what comparable others do, especially under uncertainty.
“Firms like yours chose this” reduces perceived risk.
4. Authority — people defer to credible expertise. Demonstrated competence and legitimate
credentials carry disproportionate weight.
5. Liking — people say yes more readily to those they like, and liking grows from similarity,
genuine compliments, and cooperation toward shared goals.
6. Scarcity — people value what is limited or fleeting more highly; a real deadline or limited
availability raises urgency and perceived worth.

Pitfall. The beginner uses every principle on every buyer — a fake deadline, a flattering com-
pliment, a name-drop, and a free gift all in one breath. Calibrated influence does the opposite: it
selects the few principles suited to the specific person and situation. Over-application reads as a
sales script and destroys credibility, which is the very thing authority and liking depend on.


## 2.3. Buyer cognitive biases


Quote a buyer a €2,000 “standard price” and then offer €900, and the €900 feels like a gift — even
if nothing ever sold at €2,000. Describe a treatment as “95% success” and it reassures; call the same
fact “1-in-20 failure” and it alarms. Warn a buyer that inaction will lose them €50k and they move
faster than if you promise to gain them the same €50k. Three predictable distortions are at work in
those three moments, and they shape how every buyer evaluates an offer.
Definition 7 (Anchoring, framing, loss aversion).
• Anchoring — judgments are pulled toward whatever reference number is presented first. The
opening figure in a price conversation drags the final figure toward it, even when the anchor
is arbitrary.
• Framing — the same fact is evaluated differently depending on how it is presented. “95%
success rate” and “1-in-20 failure rate” are identical facts that feel opposite.
• Loss aversion — a loss looms larger than an equivalent gain (roughly twice as large). People
work harder to avoid losing something than to gain the same thing.
Example — The three biases in a single pitch. Selling a security audit: you anchor by
mentioning that a comparable breach cost a peer firm €2M before naming your €40k fee — against
which the fee now feels small. You frame the service as “protecting the €2M you already have”
rather than “a chance to improve security,” because the protective frame engages loss aversion
— the buyer fights harder to avoid the €2M loss than to capture an abstract gain. None of this
is false; the breach cost is real and the risk is real. That is what keeps it persuasion rather than
manipulation.
Pitfall. Biases cut both ways: the buyer is anchored by your opening number, but you are
anchored by theirs. When a buyer opens with an aggressive lowball, the danger is that it silently
resets your sense of the ZOPA. Recognise the anchor as a tactic, mentally discard it, and re-
anchor against your own prepared figure rather than negotiating down from theirs.
A first ethical flag, developed fully in Chapter 9: every principle and bias in this chapter has a
manipulative twin. Anchoring on a real comparable cost is persuasion; anchoring on a fabricated

## 2.4. Resisting the same principles when they target you


A vendor buys you lunch, hands you a free trial, then asks for the order — and you feel the tug to
give something back. The defence is not to refuse the lunch; it is to ask yourself one question first:
would I want this if no favour had come with it? These principles work on you exactly as they
work on your buyer — you are the interlocutor in someone else’s negotiation more often than you
are the seller in your own — and the most reliable defence is a single prepared question for each,
one that interrupts the automatic response long enough to let you decide on the merits.
Framework 1 (One question or behaviour that resists each principle).
• Reciprocity — “Would I want this if no gift came with it?” A favour or free sample obliges
nothing; you may accept it and still decline.
• Commitment and consistency — “Would I choose this today if I had not already said yes
to the small first step?” Judge the decision on its current merits, not on staying consistent with
a foot in the door.
• Social proof — “Do those others actually share my situation and my constraints?” A crowd
that differs from you is no evidence for your choice.
• Authority — “What is the evidence, and does this person’s expertise really cover this ques-
tion?” Separate genuine competence from its trappings — a title, a confident tone, a uniform.
• Liking — “Would I accept this offer from someone I disliked?” Rate the deal, not the rapport.
• Scarcity — “Is the limit real, and would I want this regardless of the deadline?” Verify the
constraint, then decide on need, not on urgency.
The same two biases have defences too: against anchoring, name the opening number as a tactic
and re-anchor on your own prepared figure (the pitfall above); against framing, restate the fact in
the opposite frame and check whether your judgment moves; against loss aversion, ask whether
you are avoiding a loss that is real or merely one the seller has manufactured.
Exercises
2.1. What the buyer knows. You are Dpt Head at a conservative, family-owned manufacturer.
You distrust slick vendors, value the judgment of respected industry peers, and will not sign
anything without a small trial first.
What the seller knows. You sell a project-management tool. You have a case study from a
respected industrial peer, a named list of similar manufacturers already using it, and you can
offer a low-risk pilot.
Task. Write a short pitch that deliberately uses exactly three of Cialdini’s six principles, chosen
to fit this buyer. Name the three, and name the ones you deliberately left out.
Debrief. Why would using all six at once have reduced this buyer’s trust?
2.2. What the buyer knows. You are a risk-averse operations manager weighing a safety
product. You dislike gambles and hate being blamed for a preventable failure.
What the seller knows. Your product’s single factual claim is that it “works 9 times out of 10.”

2.3. What the seller knows. (You are the buyer here.) A salesperson wants to close you today.
They mention that “our biggest competitor already switched,” that “this price holds only until
Friday,” and they open by quoting a €2,000 “list price” before offering it to you for €900.
What you (the buyer) know. You have not verified any of these three claims.
Task. Name the influence principle or bias behind each of the three moves, and write the one
question you would ask yourself to resist each.
Debrief. Which of the three, if true, would be legitimate persuasion — and what single fact
would you need to check to know?
Answers. (1) No single key — check calibration: exactly three principles that genuinely fit this buyer (authority via
the peer case study, social proof via the named list, commitment via the pilot), a stated reason each fits, and at least
one deliberately left out (scarcity and heavy liking-based charm would reduce this buyer’s trust). Over-application
reads as a script and destroys the very credibility authority and liking depend on. (2) The loss frame is more persuasive
to a risk-averse buyer. Gain frame: “works 9 times out of 10”; loss frame: “fails 1 time in 10 — don’t be the one left
exposed.” Loss aversion predicts this: a prospective loss looms roughly twice as large as the equivalent gain, so the
same fact framed as a loss to be avoided engages more urgency. (3) “Competitor switched” = social proof, resisted by
“does that firm share my situation and constraints?”; “only until Friday” = scarcity, resisted by “is the deadline real,
and would I want this regardless of it?”; the €2,000 list price = anchoring, resisted by naming it as a tactic and re-
anchoring on what the thing is actually worth to you. Any of the three can be legitimate persuasion if the underlying
claim is true — the competitor really did switch, the deadline is real, €2,000 is a price the item genuinely sold at —
which is the single fact to check in each case.
Chapter takeaways. Cialdini’s six principles — reciprocity, commitment, social proof, author-
ity, liking, scarcity — are mental shortcuts buyers use, and the skill is calibration: selecting the
few that fit a specific interlocutor, not using all six at once. Three biases shape buyer judgment:
anchoring (pull toward the first number), framing (presentation changes evaluation), and loss
aversion (losses outweigh gains). Every one of these is dual-use; the line to manipulation is taken
up in Chapter 9.
