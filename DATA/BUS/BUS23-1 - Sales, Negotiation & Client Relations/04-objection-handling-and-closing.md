4. Objection Handling and Closing


## 4.1. An objection is information, not an attack


Beginners hear an objection — “it’s too expensive,” “we’re happy with our current supplier” — as
rejection, and either fold or argue. Both are wrong. An objection is the buyer telling you what
stands between them and yes. Handled well, it is the most useful moment in the conversation.
This chapter covers three techniques, and — crucially — the tactical principle behind each,
because the goal is to understand the move, not memorise a script.


## 4.2. Feel-Felt-Found


Buyer: “Honestly, we’re happy with our current supplier.” The weak reply argues straight back —
“but we do more than they do” — and the buyer digs in. The strong reply agrees first: “That makes
sense — you’ve built a relationship there, and switching is a real hassle.” Only once the buyer feels
heard does your evidence get through. That three-beat move has a name.
Definition 13 (Feel-Felt-Found). A three-step response to an objection: acknowledge the
buyer’s reaction (“I understand how you feel”), normalise it with the experience of others
(“other clients felt the same way”), and resolve it with evidence (“what they found was…”). Its
tactical principle is acknowledgment before counter: lowering the buyer’s defensiveness by
validating the objection before addressing it, so your evidence is heard rather than resisted.
Example — Feel-Felt-Found on a price objection. Buyer: “This is more expensive than the tool
we use now.” Seller: “I completely understand how you feel — the sticker price is higher (feel).
A lot of our clients felt exactly that when they compared the two (felt). What they found after
switching was that the time saved on manual reconciliation paid back the difference within four
months (found).” The objection is acknowledged, the buyer feels understood rather than argued
with, and the evidence lands.
Pitfall. Feel-Felt-Found degrades into a transparent script if recited mechanically — a buyer
who hears a robotic “I understand how you feel, others felt the same” feels handled, not heard.
The principle (acknowledge genuinely before countering) matters more than the exact words;
vary the phrasing and mean the acknowledgment.


## 4.3. Reframing


“Too expensive,” says the buyer, and the reflex is to defend the price. Instead, change what it is being
compared against: “expensive next to what — including what the unsolved problem costs you every
month it goes unfixed?” The objection was only valid inside the buyer’s original frame; move the
comparison to another frame and the answer changes.

Definition 14 (Reframing). Reframing restates an objection inside a different frame so it no
longer blocks the decision. A price objection (“too expensive”) is reframed as a value or cost-of-
inaction question (“expensive compared to what — including the cost of the problem staying
unsolved?”). The tactical principle is changing the frame of comparison: the objection was
valid only within the buyer’s original frame, so you move the comparison to a frame where the
answer differs.
This is the negotiation cousin of competitive framing from Marketing Fundamentals: a €4 energy
bar is “expensive” framed against a snack and “cheap” framed against a meal. The same product,
reframed, changes the answer to the objection.
Example — Reframing 'no budget'. Buyer: “We don’t have budget for this.” Reframe: “I hear you
— there’s no line item for it. Can I ask, what is the unsolved problem costing you each month right
now? If it’s €10k, then the real question isn’t whether there’s budget, it’s whether €10k a month is
an acceptable place to keep it.” The objection (“no budget”) is reframed as a comparison between
the cost of the tool and the cost of inaction.


## 4.4. Strategic silence


You name your price and the buyer says nothing. Three seconds stretch out, the quiet feels unbear-
able, and the untrained seller caves — “…of course, there’s some room to move” — conceding before
the buyer has said a word. The trained seller holds the silence and lets the buyer break it first.
Definition 15 (Strategic silence). Strategic silence is the deliberate withholding of speech
after you make an offer, ask a closing question, or hear an objection. Its tactical principle is
using the other party’s discomfort with silence: most people rush to fill a pause, and the
one who speaks first often concedes, reveals information, or talks themselves toward yes. Make
your statement, then stop.
Example — Silence after the price. “The investment for the full package is €60,000.” …and
then nothing. The inexperienced seller, anxious, immediately adds “…but we could probably do
something on that” — conceding before the buyer has said a word. The disciplined seller states
the number and stays quiet. The buyer fills the silence, often with useful information (“that’s a bit
more than we hoped”) that reveals where the real negotiation is.
Pitfall. The single most expensive habit in closing is talking past the close — making the ask
or stating the price and then nervously continuing, qualifying, discounting, or back-pedalling
into the silence. Ask, then be quiet. Let the other person carry the burden of the pause.

## 4.5. Getting ahead of the objection


The three moves above are reactive — they answer an objection once it lands. But you often know
what the buyer will worry about before they say it. Rather than wait for “how does this work with
our old ERP?”, the most credible sellers raise it first: “You’re probably wondering about the ERP
integration — let me deal with that now.” Naming the concern yourself signals you are not hiding it.
Definition 16 (Proactive objection management). Proactive objection management is
surfacing a concern you know the buyer is likely to have — before they raise it (“You’re probably
wondering how this handles your existing ERP; let me deal with that now”). Its tactical principle
is disarming by pre-empting: naming the concern yourself signals you are not hiding it,
defuses its emotional charge, and lets you frame it on your terms rather than on the defensive.
The sharpest form of this is admitting what your product does not do. Handled well, an honest
limitation builds credibility — it proves your other claims are not sales talk — provided you do one
thing: say for whom the limitation matters and for whom it does not. A limitation stated in
the abstract scares everyone; a limitation located (“it matters if… it never comes up if…”) lets the
right buyer see it does not apply to them, and lets the wrong buyer self-select out early, which serves
you both.
Definition 17 (Honest disclosure of a limitation). Honest disclosure names a genuine
product weakness and then locates it: the segment or use-case for whom it is a real constraint,
and the segment for whom it is irrelevant. The credibility comes from the locating — a blanket
“nothing’s perfect” concedes nothing useful; “this is a real problem if you do X, and a non-issue
if you do Y” shows you understand the buyer’s situation well enough to tell which they are.
Example — Disclosing a limitation, located. “I should be straight with you: our platform has
no offline mode — it needs a live connection. If your field teams work in warehouses or basements
with no signal, that’s a genuine constraint and you should weigh it seriously. But for an office-
based team on stable wifi, like yours, it simply never comes up.” The seller has disclosed a real
weakness, said exactly for whom it bites and for whom it does not, and — because this buyer is
in the second group — turned a confession into a proof of honesty.
Pitfall. Do not “disclose” a fake or trivial weakness to seem honest (“our only flaw is we care too
much”). Buyers see through it, and it burns the credibility the move is supposed to earn. Disclose
a real limitation, and always locate it — an unlocated admission (“it can be slow sometimes”)
just plants a doubt you never resolve.

Exercises
4.1. What the buyer knows. You run operations and are comparing tools. You have three
worries you have voiced: “too expensive,” “we’re happy with our current vendor,” and “now isn’t
a good time.”
What the seller knows. Each worry is an objection you can work, not a rejection.
Task. For each of the three objections, write one Feel-Felt-Found response and one reframe, and
name the tactical principle each relies on.
Debrief. Why does “acknowledge before counter” make Feel-Felt-Found more effective than an
immediate rebuttal? Tie it to buyer defensiveness.
4.2. What the buyer knows. You think €60,000 is more than you hoped to spend, but you have
not said so yet.
What the seller knows. You have just stated the €60,000 price and are tempted to soften it
immediately.
Task. Script the closing exchange so the seller uses strategic silence after the price. Show what
the buyer reveals by filling the pause.
Debrief. What would the seller have given away by speaking first?
4.3. What the buyer knows. Your team is office-based on stable wifi. You will ask, eventually,
whether the tool works offline.
What the seller knows. Your product has no offline mode — a real limitation, but one that
does not affect this buyer.
Task. Write how you would proactively raise the offline limitation before the buyer does,
locating for whom it matters and for whom it does not.
Debrief. Why does disclosing a genuine weakness raise credibility here — and what makes the
difference between this and a limitation left unlocated?
Answers. (1) Each objection needs one Feel-Felt-Found response (principle: acknowledge before counter) and one
reframe (principle: change the frame of comparison). Model for “too expensive”: FFF — “I understand (feel);
other clients felt that comparing sticker prices (felt); what they found was the time saved paid back the gap in months
(found)”; reframe — “expensive next to what, including the cost of leaving the problem unsolved?” On the debrief:
acknowledging first lowers defensiveness so your evidence is heard; an immediate rebuttal signals you are arguing
against the buyer, raising defensiveness so the same evidence bounces off. (2) Check that the seller states the price
and then stops, and that the buyer fills the pause with useful information — a hidden constraint, a softening (“that’s
more than we hoped”), or a concession. A model where the seller speaks first has missed the technique and would
have conceded before learning anything. (3) A strong answer raises the offline limitation unprompted, states plainly
that it is a real constraint for teams working without signal and a non-issue for an office-based team on wifi like this
one, and thereby turns the admission into proof of honesty. The difference from a weak disclosure is locating it: an
unlocated “it can be limited offline” plants a doubt and resolves nothing, while “a problem if X, irrelevant if Y — and
you’re a Y” shows you understand the buyer’s situation.
Chapter takeaways. An objection is information about what blocks the yes. React with
Feel-Felt-Found (acknowledge before countering), reframing (change the frame of com-
parison — the cousin of competitive framing), and strategic silence (let the other party’s
discomfort draw out information) — naming the principle and choosing the move, never
reciting a script, and never talking past the close. Work proactively too: raise a predictable
concern before the buyer does, and disclose a genuine limitation located for whom it matters
and for whom it does not — honesty that builds credibility.
