Preface
By the end of this course you will be able to do a set of things you cannot do yet. You will compute
in ℝ𝑛 — dot products, norms, distances, and the angle between two vectors — and bound these
quantities with the Cauchy–Schwarz and triangle inequalities; you will decide when vectors are
orthogonal, determine the orthogonal complement of a family, and build orthogonal or orthonormal
vectors by orthogonal projection and the Gram–Schmidt process. You will solve any system of linear
equations by Gaussian elimination and read off from its pivots whether the solution is unique, absent,
or a whole family. You will add and multiply matrices, invert them — a 2×2 matrix by a formula,
a larger one by Gauss–Jordan elimination — and tie invertibility to the kernel. You will recognise
a vector space and its subspaces, describe the subspace a family generates, and prove that a set is a
subspace in three standard ways. You will test a family for linear independence, extract or complete a
basis, compute a dimension, and apply the incomplete-basis theorem. You will decide whether a map
is linear, compute its kernel, image, and rank, and apply the rank theorem; you will represent a linear
map by a matrix once bases are fixed, change bases, and recognise two similar matrices as one map
seen two ways. You will decompose a space into complementary subspaces and count dimensions
with Grassmann’s formula. And throughout, you will read every algebraic fact — a kernel, a rank,
an invertibility — as a geometric feature of a transformation of space: what it collapses, what it
preserves, what it leaves fixed.
This book assumes only what Foundations built — logical reasoning, sets and functions, and the
rules of differentiation and integration used in a handful of examples; everything proper to linear
algebra is developed here from the ground up.
