7. Subspaces
In practice one rarely checks all eight axioms from scratch. Instead, most spaces of interest sit inside
a space already known to be a vector space — the solutions of 𝐴𝑋 = 0 inside ℝ𝑛, the polynomials of
degree ≤ 2 inside ℝ[𝑋]. For such subsets, a three-line test replaces the whole axiom list.


## 7.1. The three-criterion test


Definition 10. A subset 𝐹 of a vector space 𝐸 is a subspace of 𝐸 if it is itself a vector space
under the operations of 𝐸.
Theorem 4 (Three-criterion test). A subset 𝐹 ⊆ 𝐸 is a subspace if and only if
1. 0 ∈ 𝐹 (it contains the zero vector);
2. 𝐹 is closed under addition: 𝑢,𝑣 ∈ 𝐹 ⟹ 𝑢+𝑣 ∈ 𝐹;
3. 𝐹 is closed under scalar multiplication: 𝜆 ∈ ℝ and 𝑢 ∈ 𝐹 ⟹ 𝜆𝑢 ∈ 𝐹.
The test works because the eight axioms are inherited automatically from 𝐸: associativity, commu-
tativity and the rest already hold for all of 𝐸, hence for its elements in 𝐹. The only things that could
go wrong are that 𝐹 misses the zero vector or “leaks” outside itself under the operations — and that
is exactly what the three criteria forbid. Criteria 2 and 3 are often merged: 𝐹 is a subspace iff 0 ∈ 𝐹
and 𝜆𝑢+𝑣 ∈ 𝐹 for all 𝜆 ∈ ℝ, 𝑢,𝑣 ∈ 𝐹.

## 7.3. Column space and null space by row reduction


For a matrix 𝐴 the column space and null space (Chapter 4) are subspaces, and one row reduction
computes both:
• the null space is read from the parametric solution of 𝐴𝑋 = 0 — the free-variable direction vectors
span it;
• the column space is spanned by the columns of 𝐴 sitting in pivot positions (the pivot columns
of the original 𝐴).
This is the first sign that row reduction is the universal computational engine: the abstract questions
“what is the null space?” and “what is the column space?” are answered by the elimination you
already do by hand.

## 7.4. Three standard ways to prove a subspace


We now have every tool the course uses to establish that a subset is a subspace. Which one you reach
for depends only on how the subset is described.
To prove that a subset 𝐹 ⊆ 𝐸 is a subspace, use whichever fits:
1. The criteria. Verify directly that 0 ∈ 𝐹, and that 𝐹 is closed under addition and under scalar
multiplication (the three-criterion test above). Use this when 𝐹 is given by a condition on its
elements.
2. As a span. Exhibit an explicit family with 𝐹 = Span(𝑣 ,…,𝑣 ); a span is automatically a
1 𝑘
subspace. Use this when you can list generators of 𝐹.
3. As a kernel. Recognise 𝐹 as the solution set of a homogeneous system, i.e. 𝐹 = ker𝐴 for a
matrix 𝐴 (and, from Chapter 10 on, 𝐹 = ker𝑓 for a linear map 𝑓); every such set is a subspace.
Use this when 𝐹 is cut out by linear equations equal to zero.
These are three views of one fact, and they often convert into one another: the plane 𝐹 = {(𝑥,𝑦,𝑧) :
𝑥+𝑦−2𝑧 = 0} is at once a condition (method 1), a span Span((1,0,1/2),(0,1,1/2)) (method 2),
and the kernel of the 1×3 matrix (1 1 −2) (method 3). Reading a set all three ways is a core fluency
of the course.
