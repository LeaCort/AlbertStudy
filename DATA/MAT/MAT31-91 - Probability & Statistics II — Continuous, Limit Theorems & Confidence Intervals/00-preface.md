Preface
By the end of this book you will be able to work with continuous random variables — those that
take any value in an interval, such as a waiting time, a height, or a measurement error — with the
fluency you already have for counts. Concretely, you will be able to:
• evaluate the improper integrals that continuous densities require, and tell which of them converge;
• describe a continuous variable by its density and its cumulative distribution function, read proba-
bilities as areas, and compute its expectation and variance;
• compute probabilities under the uniform, exponential and normal laws — by standardizing and
reading a normal table — and recognise the chi-squared and Student-𝑡 laws as the tools for a
normal sample’s variance and small-sample mean;
• state the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem, tell the spread of a single obser-
vation, 𝜎, from the spread of an average, 𝜎/√𝑛, and use the normal approximation of the binomial;
• bound a tail probability with Chebyshev’s inequality, assuming nothing about the shape of the
distribution;
• estimate an expectation by Monte Carlo simulation and attach an honest confidence interval to
the estimate;
• build a confidence interval for a mean and read it correctly, and carry out a hypothesis test —
choosing a 𝑧- or 𝑡-test, handling one- and two-sided 𝑝-values, and avoiding the fallacies that
surround both.
These are the tools on which reported error bars, A/B tests, and every claim of statistical significance
rest.
To follow it you need two things this book takes for granted and no chapter re-teaches. From
Foundations you need to integrate fluently — the fundamental theorem of calculus, substitution,
integration by parts, and Chasles’ relation — because a continuous variable is defined and computed
entirely through integrals. From Probability & Statistics I (discrete) you need discrete probability —
random variables, expectation and variance, and the classic discrete laws — because the continuous
theory is built here as its mirror image, and almost every idea has a discrete ancestor we point back to.
